## 1. The Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus (New York Academy of Medicine)

**What it is.** A hieratic roll of **4.68 m** in **12 sheets**, **17 columns on the recto (377 lines)** and **5 on the verso (92 lines)**, written in black and red ink. The recto is a trauma treatise of **48 cases** ordered from the top of the head downward (skull, face, jaw, throat, neck vertebrae, collarbone, arm, chest, shoulder, spine); the verso holds **8 magic spells and 5 prescriptions** in a different hand. [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: Wikipedia "Edwin Smith Papyrus" dump, canonical https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Edwin_Smith_Papyrus; research ledger citing Breasted 1930, https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oip3.pdf]

- **Dates.** The surviving copy is Second Intermediate Period, Dynasties 16–17, c. 1650–1550 BC (round figure "c. 1600 BC"). The original it copies is older but the date is unresolved: Breasted argued Old Kingdom on linguistic grounds; the ledger's reading of the current literature is "Old Kingdom to 200–300 years before the copy". Breasted's suggestion that Imhotep wrote it is, in Breasted's own words, "pure conjecture", and is now regarded as highly unlikely. [ESTABLISHED for the copy; DISPUTED for the original] [MIRROR: ledger; Wikipedia dump]
- **Provenance.** Bought in **Luxor on 20 January 1862** by the American dealer-collector **Edwin Smith** from **Mustapha Agha Ayat**. After Smith's death (1906) his daughter gave it to the New-York Historical Society; it sat in the **Brooklyn Museum 1938–1948**; in **1948** it was presented to the **New York Academy of Medicine**, where it remains. [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: Wikipedia dump citing Allen p. 70 and Nunn p. 25; ledger]
- **Editions.** **James Henry Breasted**, *The Edwin Smith Surgical Papyrus*, 2 vols., University of Chicago Press, **1930** (Oriental Institute Publications 3–4), with medical advice from **Arno B. Luckhardt**. **James P. Allen**, *The Art of Medicine in Ancient Egypt* (Metropolitan Museum / Yale, **2005**), the first complete new English translation, with the first colour reproduction of the whole "fifteen-foot-long" roll, for the Met exhibition of 2005–06. **Sanchez & Meltzer**, *The Edwin Smith Papyrus: Updated Translation of the Trauma Treatise and Modern Medical Commentaries* (Lockwood Press, **2012**). [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: Wikipedia dump; PLOS ONE reference list; SNIPPET: https://www.metmuseum.org/met-publications/the-art-of-medicine-in-ancient-egypt]
- **Case architecture.** Title → examination ("If thou examinest a man having …", telling the reader where to put a hand) → diagnosis introduced by "Thou shouldst say concerning him …" → one of three verdicts → treatment → glosses explaining archaic words. Breasted's verdict wording: **"An ailment which I will treat"**, **"An ailment with which I will contend"**, **"An ailment not to be treated."** Breasted read them as the surgeon's own intended course of action, not a prediction. [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: ledger quoting Breasted p. 46–47]
- **The third verdict** occurs **fourteen times**; once (Case 17) "by evident error of the scribe"; of the remaining thirteen, ten get no treatment and three "only slight alleviatory measures not expected to cure" — "about one fourth of the material in the treatise as preserved to us". Breasted: "verdict No. 3, 'an ailment not to be treated,' is unknown in any of the other Egyptian medical papyri." [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: ledger, Breasted p. 47]
- **Modern triage claim.** Van Middendorp, Sanchez & Burridge (Eur Spine J 2010) wrote that this "therapeutic rationale … is still applied in modern military triage". The ledger flags that this is convergence, not demonstrated descent. [DISPUTED as a lineage claim] [MIRROR: ledger; SNIPPET: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/45584183]
- **Case 6 — the brain.** A gaping head wound "penetrating to the bone, smashing his skull, (and) rendering open the brain of his skull". The examiner is told he will find the smash "like those corrugations which form in molten copper", and "something therein throbbing (and) fluttering under thy fingers, like the weak place of an infant's crown before it becomes whole". Gloss B defines the copper image as "metal which the coppersmith pours off before it is [forced] into the [mould], because of something foreign upon it like [wrinkles]" — i.e. the slag skimmed off a crucible (three words in Breasted's rendering are bracketed as uncertain). Case 6 is the only case whose verdict stands without the "Thou shouldst say" formula; Breasted restores it: "[Thou shouldst say]: 'An ailment not to be treated.'" The same case gives the first written "membrane enveloping his brain" (meninges) and "fluid in the interior of his head" (cerebrospinal fluid). [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: ledger quoting Breasted's Case 6 and Gloss B; SNIPPET: https://www.acsu.buffalo.edu/~duchan/new_history/ancient_history/edwin_smith_papyrus.html ; https://historiamedica.org/sources/edwin-smith-papyrus/]
- **The word "brain".** In all Egyptian texts known in 1930 the word for brain (*ꜣmm*, conventionally "amem") was attested **eight times: seven in this papyrus, the eighth in an Ebers recipe against grey hair**. [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: ledger citing Breasted]
- **Contralateral signs.** Secondary pages attribute to Case 6 the observation that injury on one side of the head disturbs the opposite side of the body; in Breasted's numbering the "eye askew on the side of his injury" and the shuffling gait belong to Case 8 (a skull smash under intact skin, no external wound). Writers should check the case number against Breasted/Allen before quoting. [ESTABLISHED as content; attribution to case number DISPUTED between popular sources] [SNIPPET: https://www.discovermagazine.com/the-edwin-smith-papyrus-sheds-light-on-ancient-egyptian-medicine-47064 ; BKG for Case 8]
- **Case 9** is the treatise's one lapse into magic: an ostrich-eggshell dressing with a charm spoken over it, lacking observations, diagnosis and verdict; Breasted suspected a copyist inserted it. [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: ledger]
- **Case 31 — spinal cord.** "One having a dislocation in a vertebra of his neck while he is unconscious of his two legs and his arms and his urine dribbles — an ailment not to be treated." The case records paralysis of all four limbs, incontinence and priapism below a cervical injury — the earliest written description of a cord lesion. Only the three glosses follow; no treatment. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: Breasted wording as quoted at https://historiamedica.org/sources/edwin-smith-papyrus/ and https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctvvnd75 ; MIRROR: ledger]
- **Case 48 and the ending.** The copy stops mid-sentence in Case 48 (a spinal injury), with the rubric written and, in Breasted's description, the pen still flooded with ink — read as a sudden interruption. A blank space follows, then the verso's spells, which Breasted called "a hodge-podge of the customary magical recipes, drawn from the traditional folk medicine inherited from the superstition of a remote past" (p. xvii). [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: ledger; https://github.com/KairoiAI/An_Incomplete_History_of_Research_Ethics quoting Breasted]
- **Treatments recorded across the cases:** stitching of wounds, honey and grease dressings, fresh meat applied on the first day (haemostasis), splints and immobilisation for head and spine injuries, "mooring" the patient "at his mooring stakes" (bed rest) until the injury passes. [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: Wikipedia dump; BKG for the mooring-stakes phrase — verify wording in Breasted]
- **Fringe.** Claims that the papyrus records brain or heart surgery are false: penetrating brain injuries are the very cases marked "not to be treated". [FRINGE] [MIRROR: compiled notes at https://github.com/AlienLifeShop/TheoriesOfAnything, §4.1 — a tertiary compilation, used only for the framing]

## 2. The Ebers Papyrus and the Egyptian heart; the other medical papyri; Teti Neb Fu

### 2.1 Ebers (Leipzig University Library)
- **What it is.** A single roll of roughly **20 m** in **110 columns ("pages")**, the longest and most complete Egyptian medical text: **877 numbered sections/remedies** (older counts say "~700"). A calendar note on the verso dates the copy to **year 9 of Amenhotep I**, c. **1550 BC** (some tables give 1536 BC). Purchased at Luxor by **Georg Ebers** in the winter of **1872–73** (sources vary: "1872", "1873", "1873–74"); it had earlier passed through Edwin Smith's hands. Kept at the **University of Leipzig Library**. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebers_Papyrus ; https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=1353 ; MIRROR: compiled notes citing Ebbell 1937]
- **Translations.** Joachim 1890 (German); Cyril Bryan 1930 (English, from the German); Bendix Ebbell 1937 (*The Papyrus Ebers: The Greatest Egyptian Medical Document*); Paul Ghalioungui 1987 (Cairo); Reinhold Scholl 2002 (Leipzig). Quote from Ebbell or Ghalioungui, or from Nunn 1996, not from Bryan. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: Wikipedia; Amazon listing of Bryan; MIRROR: compiled bibliography]
- **Contents beyond the heart:** hundreds of prescriptions for eye, skin, gut, dental and gynaecological complaints; a section on **mental disorders including depression**; extraction of the **Guinea worm** by winding it on a stick; contraception; tumours; "too plentiful urine" read by some as diabetes. [ESTABLISHED, with the diabetes reading DISPUTED] [SNIPPET: Wikipedia; https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.13070860]
- **The "treatise on the heart" (Ebers 854–856).** The heart is "the centre of the blood supply, with vessels attached for every member of the body". Ebers **854a–o** lists the *metu* (vessels/channels) attached to the heart, numbering **12 or 22** depending on the passage; **856b**: "As to a man, there are 22 metu in him, to his heart. It is they which give to all his members." Some of these metu carry blood; others carry air, water, mucus, semen and (in the pathological theory) *wekhedu*, the putrefying matter that rises from the bowel and causes disease. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: Wikipedia; https://onlinejcf.com/article/S1071-9164(06)00135-7/abstract ; BKG for wekhedu: Nunn, *Ancient Egyptian Medicine*, 1996, ch. 3]
- **The heart "speaks".** The opening of the treatise, in Nunn's rendering (Nunn 1996, p. 55, after Ebers 854a): "The beginning of the physician's secret: knowledge of the heart's movement and knowledge of the heart. There are vessels from it to every limb. As to this, when any physician, any Sekhmet-priest or any exorcist places his hands or his fingers upon the head, upon the back of the head, upon the hands, upon the place of the stomach, upon the arms or upon the feet, then he examines the heart, because all his limbs possess its vessels, that is: it speaks out of the vessels of every limb." This is the pulse, taken at the same sites a modern clinician uses. [ESTABLISHED as content; wording BKG — verify against Nunn before quoting on screen]
- **Heart failure in Egyptian words.** Ebers 855: when the heart is "weak/faint", "this means that the heart does not speak, or that the vessels of the heart are dumb". Other 855 glosses attribute forgetfulness, anger, "flooding" of the heart, and a heart that "kneels" to disorders of the *metu*. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://onlinejcf.com/article/S1071-9164(06)00135-7/abstract ; https://psychiatryonline.org/doi/10.1176/appi.ajp.2013.13070860]
- **Heart, mind and mummification.** For the Egyptians the heart (*ib*, *haty*) was the seat of thought and memory; the brain had no known function and was removed and discarded in mummification, while the heart was left in place (or returned) to be weighed against Maat's feather in the judgment (Book of the Dead spell 125) and protected by the heart scarab (spell 30B). See `01` §18 and `05` A2. [ESTABLISHED] [BKG; cross-ref]
- **Smith papyrus on the pulse.** Case 1 of the Smith papyrus already glosses "counting" the pulse: the physician "measures" the heart because "there are vessels in it leading to every limb", "as one counts with a measuring vessel". [ESTABLISHED as content; wording BKG — Breasted Case 1, Gloss A]

### 2.2 Kahun, Hearst, London, Berlin, Brooklyn
- **Kahun (Lahun) Gynaecological Papyrus, UC32057, Petrie Museum UCL.** Found by Flinders Petrie at El-Lahun (Faiyum) in **1889**; restored 1890 and published by F. Ll. Griffith in **1898**; dated c. **1825 BC** (12th Dynasty, reign of Amenemhat III) — the oldest surviving medical papyrus. **34 sections**, each a case of diagnosis and treatment (no prognosis), nearly every ailment traced to the womb and treated by fumigation, potions, or massage. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kahun_Gynaecological_Papyrus ; https://www.intechopen.com/chapters/78710 ; MIRROR: Wikipedia "Kahun Papyri" dump citing http://www.digitalegypt.ucl.ac.uk/med/birthpapyrus.html]
- **Hearst Medical Papyrus, Bancroft Library, Berkeley.** Handed to George Reisner's Hearst Expedition camp in spring **1901** by a villager of **Deir el-Ballas**; **18 columns**, about **260 prescriptions** (urinary, blood, hair, bites), c. 1450 BC; Reisner published it in 17 facsimile plates (1905). [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hearst_papyrus ; https://digicoll.lib.berkeley.edu/record/237827]
- **London Medical Papyrus, BM EA 10059.** **61 recipes, 25 medical, the rest magical**; skin, eye, bleeding (to prevent miscarriage), burns; New Kingdom (c. 1300 BC). It contains spells transcribed in foreign languages (readings of "Cretan" and Semitic passages are debated). [ESTABLISHED; foreign-language readings DISPUTED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/London_Medical_Papyrus ; https://sae.saw-leipzig.de/en/documents/london-medical-papyrus]
- **Berlin Medical Papyrus 3038 ("Brugsch Papyrus").** 19th-Dynasty hand, c. 1350–1200 BC; fertility and contraception; the **barley-and-emmer pregnancy test**: the woman urinates daily on two bags of grain; growth means pregnancy, barley first a boy, emmer a girl. Ghalioungui, Khalil & Ammar (1963) found pregnant women's urine promoted germination in about 70% of cases, non-pregnant urine in none — but the sex prediction failed. A Ptolemaic-era Demotic copy of the same test surfaced in the Carlsberg papyrus collection, Copenhagen, in 2018 (Sofie Schiødt). [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Brugsch_Papyrus ; https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/egyptian-papyrus-reveals-old-wives-tale-very-very-old-indeed-180970066/ ; MIRROR: compiled notes citing Ghalioungui 1963]
- **Brooklyn "snakebite" papyrus, Brooklyn Museum 47.218.48 + 47.218.85.** A handbook for the **priests of Serqet** (the scorpion goddess who guarded against venom): the first part (to §38) catalogues **38 reptiles** (24 registers survive), noting which bites kill, which are dangerous, and which are harmless; the second part gives about **140 treatments** in a fixed pattern (indication, ingredients, preparation, administration), including onion remedies. The surviving copy is Late Period (c. 660–330 BC; Wikipedia gives "c. 450 BC"), of an older text. First edited in French by **Serge Sauneron**, published posthumously **1989**; new study by Sanchez, Meltzer, Wüster, Casewell & Schuett, *Snake Identification in the Ancient Egyptian Brooklyn Medical Papyrus* (Lockwood, 2024). [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/14/10/1300 ; https://www.lockwoodpress.com/product-page/snake-identification-in-the-ancient-egyptian-brooklyn-medical-papyrus-a-new ; MIRROR: Wikipedia "Snakebite" dump]

### 2.3 The tomb of Teti Neb Fu (announced 6 January 2025)
- A joint **French–Swiss mission** digging in **south Saqqara** since 2022 for officials of **Pepi II** (6th Dynasty, c. 2280–2180 BC; the syndicated report gives Britannica's 2305–2118 BC) found the **mastaba** of **Teti Neb Fu**. His titles on the sarcophagus and walls: **chief physician of the palace**, **priest and "magician" of Serket** (i.e. a venom specialist), **chief dentist**, and **director of medicinal plants** — only the second attestation of that last title, and "chief dentist" is itself rare. The tomb had been looted in antiquity but keeps a painted false door, offering scenes, an inscribed stone sarcophagus and a ceiling painted to imitate granite. The Ministry of Tourism and Antiquities announced it in a Facebook post on 6 Jan 2025: "A joint French-Swiss archaeological mission has uncovered the mastaba tomb of a royal physician named 'Teti Neb Fu' … dating back to the Old Kingdom during the reign of King Pepi II." [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: phys.org / News Tribune report by Irene Wright, 7 Jan 2025, https://phys.org/news/2025-01-ancient-doctor-expert-venomous-egypt.html ; SNIPPET: https://archaeologymag.com/2025/01/tomb-of-royal-physician-discovered-in-egypt/ ; https://www.heritagedaily.com/2025/01/mastaba-tomb-of-teti-neb-fu-discovered-in-the-saqqara-region/154263]
- The Brooklyn papyrus, a priest of Serket in a 6th-Dynasty tomb, and Serket among the four goddesses guarding Tutankhamun's canopic shrine (see `01`) form one continuous "venom-medicine" thread across 2,000 years. [ESTABLISHED] [cross-ref]

## 3. Prostheses, dentistry, amputation, trepanation

### 3.1 The Cairo toe (Egyptian Museum, Cairo)
- Found on the mummified **right foot** of **Tabaketenmut**, daughter of a priest, in a re-used burial in Theban tomb **TT95** at Sheikh Abd el-Qurna; dated **c. 950–710 BC** (21st–22nd Dynasty). Made in **three pieces**, two of **wood** and one possibly of **leather**, jointed so the toe can flex, with holes for lacing to the foot; it shows wear. Nerlich et al. first described it in *The Lancet* 356 (2000) 2176–79, "Ancient Egyptian prosthesis of the big toe" (DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(00)03507-8), and proposed ischaemic gangrene from diabetes as the reason for the amputation, with the stump healed before the toe was fitted. [ESTABLISHED; the diabetes cause is a hypothesis] [SNIPPET: https://www.thelancet.com/journals/lancet/article/PIIS0140673611601906/fulltext ; https://www.natureasia.com/en/nmiddleeast/article/10.1038/nmiddleeast.2012.161 ; MIRROR: DOI in compiled bibliography]
- A **2017** re-examination by the University of Basel / Egyptian Museum team working in TT95 (microscopy, X-ray, CT) reported that the toe had been **re-fitted and adjusted several times** to the wearer's foot. [ESTABLISHED per press coverage; details UNVERIFIED here] [SNIPPET, title only: https://www.thehistoryblog.com/archives/47762 ; https://www.ancient-origins.net/artifacts-ancient-technology/new-revelations-when-3000-year-old-prosthetic-toe-examined-cutting-edge-021453]

### 3.2 The Greville Chester toe (British Museum EA 29996)
- A **right big toe with part of the forefoot** in **cartonnage** (linen, glue and plaster), dated **before 600 BC**, said to come from Thebes, bought for the museum in **1881** by the Rev. Greville Chester. It once carried a false nail and shows wear, but does not bend, so it was probably more cosmetic than the Cairo toe. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA29996 ; https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/mummies-false-toes-helped-ancient-egyptians-walk/]

### 3.3 Finch's gait tests — the earliest functional prosthesis
- **Jacky Finch** (KNH Centre for Biomedical Egyptology, University of Manchester) set out the case in *The Lancet* 377 (12 Feb 2011) 548–49, "The ancient origins of prosthetic medicine" (DOI 10.1016/S0140-6736(11)60190-6), calling the Cairo toe "possibly the oldest known intravital limb prosthesis". [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: DOI in two bibliographies; SNIPPET: Lancet page]
- In **2012** (Journal of Prosthetics and Orthotics) she reported tests with **two volunteers** who had lost the right big toe, wearing exact **replicas** of both toes with replica Egyptian sandals in the University of Salford gait laboratory; pressure and comfort were measured. Both replicas let the volunteers walk; the wooden Cairo design worked best and was reported as comfortable, the cartonnage toe "very satisfactory" with a sandal. Press coverage quoted figures near 87% (Cairo) and 78% (cartonnage) of normal toe flexion — **check the exact percentages in the Manchester release before use.** Finch: the toes are "beautifully fashioned with a real eye to anatomical detail". [ESTABLISHED; percentages UNVERIFIED] [SNIPPET: https://www.manchester.ac.uk/about/news/egyptian-toes-likely-to-be-the-worlds-oldest-prosthetics/ ; https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/781138 ; https://www.cbsnews.com/news/false-mummy-toes-owners-walked-like-egyptians/]
- Both toes predate the **Capua leg** (c. 300 BC, Roman, bronze and wood, destroyed in the WWII bombing of the Royal College of Surgeons), previously the oldest known prosthesis. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: Manchester; MIRROR: Wikipedia "Prosthesis" dump]

### 3.4 The Giza gold-wire "bridge" and Egyptian dentistry
- In **1914 Hermann Junker** found at Giza, loose in the debris of a burial, **two lower molars (left second and third) bound together with gold wire**. Junker first reported the wiring as **post-mortem** (done during mummification); by 1929 he accepted **Euler**'s view that the wire had held a loose tooth in life. A second appliance from **El-Qatta (1952, Farid)** joins three teeth. Recent reviews (Pantazis et al., *Anatomical Record* 2020) lean back to funerary work: the appliances show no evidence of long use in the mouth. Photographs that circulate online as "ancient Egyptian false teeth" show a 1900s replica. [DISPUTED — genuinely open] [SNIPPET: https://anatomypubs.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ar.24487 ; https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12789021/ ; https://africacheck.org/fact-checks/meta-programme-fact-checks/ancient-egyptians-did-practise-dentistry-photos-show-1900s]
- A Giza mandible of c. 2650 BC has **two holes drilled below the root of the first molar**, read since Hooton (1917) as drainage of an abscess; others read them as natural fistulae. [DISPUTED] [MIRROR: Wikipedia "Surgery" dump; BKG for Hooton]
- Egyptian teeth were ground down by sand in bread flour; abscesses and periodontal disease are near-universal in mummies, which is why "chief dentist" was a real court office (Hesy-Ra, Teti Neb Fu). [ESTABLISHED] [MIRROR: https://github.com/JanHerca/urantiapedia (a secondary essay); SNIPPET: PMC dentistry review]

### 3.5 Amputation, trepanation, false eyes
- **Healed amputations** are documented: Dupras et al., "Evidence of amputation as medical treatment in ancient Egypt" (Int. J. Osteoarchaeology 2010, Dakhleh Oasis); an Old Kingdom series in which "the distal ends of all amputated bones were well-healed, suggesting that they lived long enough after the amputation"; and a 2022 on-site radiographic case from an Egyptian necropolis. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://www.researchgate.net/publication/229913806 ; https://www.researchgate.net/publication/261804913 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC9809921/]
- **Trepanation** is common in prehistoric Europe and Peru but **rare in Egypt**; a handful of skulls have been claimed and most are disputed. The Smith papyrus never prescribes opening the skull; "~70% survival" figures in popular compilations are world figures, not Egyptian. [ESTABLISHED as rarity] [MIRROR: compiled notes; BKG: Nunn 1996]
- **False eyes.** From the 21st Dynasty embalmers packed the face and set artificial eyes (stone, glass, or painted linen) under the lids; Ramesses IV's orbits held small onions. These are cosmetic restorations for the afterlife, not aids to the living. [ESTABLISHED] [BKG: Ikram & Dodson, *The Mummy in Ancient Egypt*, 1998]
- **Artificial limbs on mummies.** Apart from the two toes, no securely functional limb prosthesis is known from Egypt; a few mummies have wooden or reed "limbs" restoring a lost part for burial. Treat any "mummy with a wooden leg" story as [UNVERIFIED].

## 4. Practitioners

- **swnw** (fem. **swnwt**) is the word for physician; the sign group is an arrow over a pot, which gave rise to the (unproven) idea that the first doctors were "arrow-pullers". Ranks: *wr swnw* "chief physician", *imy-r swnw* "overseer of physicians", *swnw pr-ꜥꜣ* "physician of the Great House (palace)"; specialists include eye, belly and tooth doctors and the "shepherd of the anus" — Herodotus 2.84 confirms the specialisation ("each physician is for one disease and not for more"). Priests of Sekhmet and lector-priests also treated the sick. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ancient_Egyptian_medicine ; BKG: Nunn 1996; Herodotus text in `05` A1]
- **Hesy-Ra** (Hesire), 3rd Dynasty under Djoser, c. 2650 BC: mastaba **S2405** at Saqqara, found by Mariette in 1861 and excavated by **Quibell 1911–12**; its **cedar-wood panels** (Cairo Museum, newly re-exhibited) carry his titles, among them **wr-ibḥ-swnw, "great one of the dentists/physicians"** — "the greatest of the physicians who treat teeth" — making him the earliest named doctor and dentist. He was also chief of the royal scribes. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Hesy-Ra ; https://english.ahram.org.eg/NewsContent/9/40/533402/Antiquities/Ancient-Egypt/Egyptian-Museum-in-Tahrir-Unveils-New-Exhibition-o.aspx ; https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?entryid=3624]
- **Peseshet**, 4th (or 5th) Dynasty, c. 2500 BC: known from a false door/stela in the Giza tomb of her son **Akhethetep**, with the title **imy-r swnwt, "overseer of female physicians"** — the earliest named woman in medicine, and proof of a body of women doctors to oversee; whether she practised herself is unknown. ## 5. Paleopathology

- **Ruffer 1910.** Sir Marc Armand Ruffer, "Note on the presence of *Bilharzia haematobia* in Egyptian mummies of the Twentieth Dynasty", *BMJ* 1 (2557), 1910, p. 16 (DOI 10.1136/bmj.1.2557.16-a): **calcified Schistosoma haematobium eggs in the kidneys of two 20th-Dynasty mummies** (c. 1250–1000 BC) — the first parasite identified in ancient tissue, made possible by Ruffer's rehydration technique for mummy tissue. Ruffer coined "palaeopathology" (1913) and died at sea in 1917. - **Ramesses V (reigned c. 1149–1145 BC).** Mummy recovered from the KV35 cache by Loret (1898), unwrapped by G. Elliot Smith (1905): a young man "full of disease" with a **pustular rash on face, neck, shoulders**; Ruffer & Ferguson (1911) sectioned skin from a 20th-Dynasty mummy and found dome-shaped vesicles "similar to those found in smallpox"; Donald Hopkins examined the mummy in 1979 but was not allowed to remove pustules, and electron microscopy of shroud tissue failed. No variola DNA has ever been recovered; buboes in the groin have prompted a bubonic-plague alternative; genomic work dates the modern smallpox clade far later. Burial was delayed to Year 2 of Ramesses VI. [DISPUTED — plausible, unconfirmed] [MIRROR: Wikipedia "Ramesses V" dump; SNIPPET: WHO *Smallpox and its Eradication* ch. 5, https://biotech.law.lsu.edu/blaw/bt/smallpox/who/red-book/9241561106_chp5.pdf]
- **KV60 / "Hatshepsut".** Carter found KV60 in 1903 with two female mummies; KV60B lay in a coffin of the wet-nurse **Sitre-In**; KV60A, obese, lay on the floor. Elizabeth Thomas suggested in 1966 that KV60A might be Hatshepsut. In **June 2007 Zahi Hawass** announced the identification: a CT of a wooden box from the DB320 royal cache, inscribed for Hatshepsut and holding embalmed viscera, revealed a **molar lacking one root**, said to fit the empty socket in KV60A's jaw "within a fraction of a millimetre". DNA testing was refused because it would destroy the tooth. In **2011** the box tooth was re-identified as a **lower** molar while KV60A is missing an **upper** one, which would break the match. Hawass also proposed diabetes, bone cancer and a carcinogenic skin lotion. [DISPUTED — the identification is argued, not settled] [MIRROR: Wikipedia "Hatshepsut" dump (two versions); SNIPPET: https://www.guardians.net/hawass/Press%20Releases/identifying_hatshepsut.htm ; https://advancingtheresearch.org/wp-content/uploads/2026/04/Hatshepsut07.pdf]
- **Malaria at Amarna.** In the **South Tombs Cemetery** (Amarna Project, Barry Kemp, with the University of Arkansas and Southern Illinois University) about **half** the skeletons show lesions consistent with recent malarial infection (cribra orbitalia, porotic hyperostosis, periostitis), and malarial individuals died younger; the burden fell on women and children. A 2016 pilot found the same at the North Tombs Cemetery (Dabbs et al.). An *American Journal of Archaeology* paper (Oct 2025), "Mortality Crisis at Akhetaten?", reads the cemetery demography as a Late Bronze Age epidemic. Tutankhamun's own *P. falciparum* DNA (JAMA 2010) is in `01`. ## Film hooks (fact → fiction; ⟂ marks where the truth ends)

1. **The three verdicts as the ASI's operating law.** Real: the Smith papyrus sorts every patient into treat / contend / not treat, and a quarter of cases are abandoned. Fiction: the ASI, reading the papyrus as its founding medical text, applies the same triage to humanity. ⟂ Nothing in the papyrus applies to populations, and the "modern triage" descent is a modern analogy, not history.
2. **"An ailment not to be treated" — then treated.** Real: Case 6 (open skull, exposed brain) and Case 31 (cervical cord) are marked hopeless. Fiction: the ASI's first act of hubris is to take Case 6 and Case 31 patients — the two verdicts Breasted could only read as death — and cure them, before it turns to the boy king. ⟂ No Egyptian surgeon operated on brain or cord.
3. **Brain like copper slag.** Real: the oldest word for "brain" sits beside an image of the scum a coppersmith skims from molten metal. Fiction: the ASI's substrate is copper; when Tut's skull is opened the machine sees its own reflection in Gloss B. ⟂ Poetry; the papyrus does not say the brain is copper.
4. **The heart speaks in the vessels — and Tut has no heart.** Real: Ebers 854–856 treats the pulse as the heart "speaking" in 22 channels to every limb; "the heart does not speak" is the Egyptian definition of dying; the heart is what is weighed at judgment; Tut's mummy has none. Fiction: the cyborg has pulse telemetry in every limb but no heart to originate it — by Egyptian theology an unjudgeable being, neither living nor dead, which is exactly what the ASI wants. ⟂ The metu are not a circulatory system in the modern sense (they also carry air, mucus, semen and disease), and the film should not claim Egyptians "discovered circulation".
5. **The Cairo toe as the first component.** Real: a hinged wooden toe, laced to a healed stump, refitted several times, worn by a priest's daughter c. 900 BC, and shown by gait tests to work. Fiction: the ASI's first prosthesis for Tut's necrotic foot is a deliberate copy of Tabaketenmut's toe in titanium, screwed into bone (OPRA). ⟂ No Egyptian prosthesis was ever anchored in bone; osseointegration dates from 1965 (teeth) and 1990 (limbs).
6. **The 2015 titanium chest.** Real: a CT-scanned, electron-beam-printed titanium sternum and ribs already exist, made in Melbourne for a patient in Salamanca. Fiction: Tut's chest — missing sternum and front ribs (see `01` §5) — is closed with the same part, scaled from his 1968/2005 CT data. ⟂ Only the chest wall is real; no printed part houses a heart.
7. **Teti Neb Fu's tomb as a real 2025 location.** Real: a 6th-Dynasty palace physician-magician-dentist-herbalist whose mastaba was announced in January 2025. Fiction: the resistance's scientists use the freshly opened tomb, and its "director of medicinal plants" pharmacopoeia, as their House of Life. ⟂ No medical papyrus was found in the tomb.
8. **The venom priesthood.** Real: the Brooklyn papyrus is the manual of the priests of Serqet; Teti Neb Fu was one; Serqet guards Tut's canopic shrine. Fiction: a lineage of Serqet-priests who "treat what bites" becomes the human faction that knows how to poison a machine. ⟂ Invented lineage.
9. **Peseshet, not Merit Ptah.** Real: the overseer of women physicians c. 2500 BC is real; "Merit Ptah" is a 1938 mistake. Fiction: the film's lead surgeon is named Peseshet; a rival AI cites "Merit Ptah" and is caught out — a test of who reads sources. ⟂ Nothing more is known of Peseshet than her title.
10. **Imhotep's empty tomb.** Real: Imhotep's tomb has never been found; he became Asklepios; he was never provably a doctor. Fiction: the ASI's Saqqara base is the sought-after tomb. ⟂ Pure invention (see `07` for the Saqqara geography).
11. **The KV60 tooth.** Real: a whole royal identification hangs on a single molar that may be from the wrong jaw. Fiction: the ASI identifies bodies the same way, from teeth and DNA it will not let humans check. ⟂ The film should keep Hatshepsut's identification "argued, not settled".
12. **Ruffer's eggs.** Real: the first parasite found in ancient tissue was in a 20th-Dynasty kidney. Fiction: the ASI rehydrates Tut's tissue exactly as Ruffer did and reads his malaria and schistosomes as an old-world "firmware". ⟂ Tut's own kidneys were not examined for eggs; his malaria is DNA-based (`01`).
