## 1. The Dendera zodiac (Louvre D 38)

**What it is**
- A sandstone bas-relief sky map cut from the ceiling of the second eastern Osiris chapel on the roof of the Temple of Hathor at Dendera (Upper Egypt). The disk of the sky is carried by four standing goddesses and eight falcon-headed figures; a second, *linear* (rectangular) zodiac remains in situ on the ceiling of the temple's pronaos. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT Priskin, figs 1–2] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera_zodiac
- Louvre inventory **D 38** (the Wikimedia Commons file also carries "E 13482"); block 254 × 254.25 cm, the circle c. 2.55 m across; displayed in room 325, Sully wing, first floor. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET] https://collections.louvre.fr/en/ark:/53355/cl010028871 ; https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Zodiaque_de_Dend%C3%A9ra_-_Mus%C3%A9e_du_Louvre_Antiquit%C3%A9s_Egyptiennes_D_38_;_E_13482.jpg

**Discovery, drawing, removal**
- 1799: Vivant Denon, travelling with Desaix's column during Bonaparte's expedition, sketched the ceiling; published in his *Voyage dans la Basse et la Haute Égypte* (1802). The engineers Jollois and Devilliers' drawing in the *Description de l'Égypte* reproduced the ceiling *with* the surrounding cartouches — which Lelorrain's saws later left behind on the wall. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET victorianweb; BKG for Denon's exact route] https://www.victorianweb.org/science/denderazodiac.html ; https://www.lindahall.org/experience/digital-exhibitions/napoleon-and-the-scientific-expedition-to-egypt/14-the-zodiac-of-dendera/
- 1820–21: the antiquities dealer Sébastien-Louis Saulnier hired the engineer Lelorrain (given as Claude in most sources, Jean-Baptiste in one) to cut the relief out "with saws, jacks, scissors and gunpowder"; it reached France in 1821. Louis XVIII paid **150,000 francs** and installed it in the Bibliothèque royale (1822); it passed to the Louvre in 1922 (one student page says 1907 — treat 1922 as standard). [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT: louvre-visit-guide mirror; SNIPPET: victorianweb, Wikipedia] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera_zodiac ; https://kerrysloft.com/history-of-science/saulnier-1822-on-the-temple-of-dendera/

**The "Dendera affair" (age dispute, 1800s)**
- Joseph Fourier estimated c. 2500 BC; enthusiasts, using precession as the lever, pushed it to the 15th millennium BC or beyond (Dupuis; "17,000 years" in some accounts) — i.e., older than Genesis, which is why the Church cared. Jean-Baptiste Biot argued c. 700 BC (often given as 716 BC) in papers of the early 1820s. Georges Cuvier put it at AD 123–147. [ESTABLISHED as history of the debate] [SNIPPET] https://www.victorianweb.org/science/denderazodiac.html ; https://kids.kiddle.co/Dendera_zodiac
- Champollion, using his new decipherment, read the Greek word *autokrator* ("emperor") in one of the cartouches drawn in the *Description* (correcting Thomas Young's reading "Arsinoë") and placed the ceiling in the Greco-Roman period; he also showed that the little stars after certain signs were determinatives, not astronomical marks. This is the origin of the modern date. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET] https://www.victorianweb.org/science/denderazodiac.html

**Modern dating (Aubourg 1995) and its critics**
- Éric Aubourg, "La date de conception du zodiaque du temple d'Hathor à Dendérah", *BIFAO* 95 (1995) 1–10: the five planets are drawn at (or near) their stationary points in the constellations where they stood in summer 50 BC — a configuration recurring roughly once a millennium — and two disks mark eclipses: a **solar eclipse of 7 March 51 BC** (a disk containing Isis holding the baboon Thoth by the tail) and a **lunar eclipse of 25 September 52 BC** (an Eye of Horus in a disk). Sylvie Cauville, *Le zodiaque d'Osiris* (Peeters 1997; corrected 2nd ed. 2015). [ESTABLISHED as the standard date; details DISPUTED] [SNIPPET Wikipedia; V-TEXT Priskin fig. 3] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dendera_zodiac ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C3%89ric_Aubourg
- Priskin (ENIM 8, 2015) sets out the two scholarly camps: Aubourg–Cauville read the zodiac as "a 'snapshot' of the sky taken at around the time of the construction of the Osirian chapels"; Christian Leitz (building on Neugebauer & Parker) reads it as "a comprehensive catalogue of constellations … compiled regardless of time". Priskin's objection to the snapshot: the planets sit in their traditional *hypsomata* (exaltations), an astrological convention, so their positions cannot prove a date; he nevertheless accepts the two eclipse identifications and asks why the solar eclipse of 21 August 50 BC is *not* shown. [DISPUTED] [V-TEXT priskin_dendera.md lines 38, 63]

**What is Babylonian/Greek and what is native**
- Clagett: "The pictorial representations of the zodiacal signs in the Egyptian zodiacs (though not their full range of Hellenistic additions) were surely of ultimate Babylonian origin. … The Egyptian zodiacs not only included the signs of the zodiac but the old hour decans, the planets, and other Egyptian stars or constellations as well (like the Big Dipper and the Hippopotamus)." The zodiac reaches Egypt only in Ptolemaic times; the earliest Egyptian zodiac is the rectangular one from Esna ("Esna A"). [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett_vol2.md lines 1488–1495]
- Native elements on the Dendera disk: the **36 decans** around the rim; **Meskhetiu** (the Bull's Foreleg = Ursa Major/Big Dipper, the one circumpolar figure everyone agrees on); the **Hippopotamus** (Taweret-type figure, holding a mooring post; Pogo placed her stars in Draco); **Sah** (Orion as Osiris) and **Sopdet** (Sirius as a recumbent cow in a barque) followed by the cataract goddesses Satet and Anuket, who "signify the advent of the annual flooding of the Nile". [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT Priskin lines 38, 236, 297, 390; Clagett 1367–1420]
- The exaltation system itself is by consensus Babylonian (Rochberg-Halton, *JAOS* 108, 1988), though Priskin argues some of it could be Egyptian. [DISPUTED] [V-TEXT Priskin line 222 and n. 116]
- Practical note for the writers: no degrees. Clagett: "there was no use of a system of degrees in ancient Egypt to measure celestial arcs." [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT line 1489]

## 2. The 36 decans: star tables, Senenmut, Ramesside star clocks, and the Book of Nut's 70 days

**Diagonal star tables (Middle Kingdom coffin lids)**
- Painted on the undersides of wooden coffin lids, mostly from Asyut, 9th–12th Dynasties (from c. 2150 BC). 24 lids were known; a table found in the Mallawi museum (published *JHA* 44, 2013) makes 25, and Cockcroft & Symons (*JHA* 45, 2014) added a fragment and revised tables S16C and S1C. First systematic edition: Neugebauer & Parker, *Egyptian Astronomical Texts I: The Early Decans* (Brown Univ./Lund Humphries 1960), followed by vol. II *The Ramesside Star Clocks* (1964) and vol. III *Decans, Planets, Constellations and Zodiacs* (1969). [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET] https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2013JHA....44..457S/abstract ; https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/002182861404500204 ; https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1969eati.book.....N/abstract
- Structure (Parker, as quoted by Clagett): read right to left; a top "date line" runs from the 1st decade of the 1st month to the 36th decade; "with decade 26 there begins in the 12th hour a triangle of alternate decans to tell the hours through the epagomenal days"; 12 rows = 12 night hours; each decan rises one hour earlier every ten days, so "a star in the 12th hour rises over 120 days to the first hour and then drops out of the clock." A strip between columns 18 and 19 on some coffins shows Nut, the Foreleg (Big Dipper), Orion looking back, and Sothis. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett lines 657–668, 705–710]
- Clagett: "It seems certain that the division of night into twelve hours arose from the 12-month year and the 10-day week and thus was a consequence of the installation and acceptance of the civil calendar." Parker: "It was not until roughly 2150 B.C. that we know for certain that these night hours totalled 12." The decanal 12-hour night is the accepted root of our 24-hour day. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT lines 649–657] ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decan
- Caveat (Antognini, following the specialists): all surviving tables are funerary and schematic; "there has actually been some debate as to whether or not these so-called diagonal star clocks were really clocks at all." [DISPUTED] [V-TEXT urania_032.md]

**Senenmut's ceiling (TT 353) — the earliest surviving celestial diagram**
- Found January 1927 (1926/27 season) by Herbert Winlock for the Metropolitan Museum's Egyptian Expedition at Deir el-Bahri: an unfinished, never-used tomb reached by a c. 90 m descending stair under Hatshepsut's temple; the ceiling is in the chamber "at the bottom of the third flight of stairs". Dated to Hatshepsut's reign, c. 1479–1458 BC (the brief's "c. 1470 BC"). Not to be confused with Senenmut's official tomb TT 71 at Sheikh Abd el-Qurna. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 1330–1340; SNIPPET] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_ceiling_of_Senenmut%27s_Tomb ; https://www.maat-ka-ra.de/english/personen/senenmut/sen_t353.htm ; https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/544566
- Layout: a **southern panel** listing the decans (with Orion, Sothis/Sirius, the "triangle" decans, and the planets Jupiter, Saturn, Mercury, Venus — **Mars is missing**, a famous puzzle) and a **northern panel** with the circumpolar constellations flanked at the top by twelve circles labelled with the month names (each cut into 24 sectors; Clagett suggests they held 12-hour transit lists for the 1st and 16th of each month) and at the bottom by the day-deities. Five columns of prayers for Senenmut, including Hatshepsut's titulary, separate the panels. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 1340, 1357, 1433; SNIPPET arXiv 0801.1331] https://arxiv.org/pdf/0801.1331
- Northern constellations: **Meskhetyu** (Big Dipper) drawn as a bull whose tail ends in a circled star — Pogo identified it with Zeta Ursae Majoris (Mizar); two cords run from it down to the horizon (meridian cords, later read as reins); Serket (scorpion goddess) stands behind; **Anu**, a falcon-headed god, spears or holds a cord to the bull; the **Hippopotamus** with a crocodile on her back (Pogo: stars in Draco), a crocodile lunging at a **Man**, a Divine Lion, and two crocodile figures (Haqu, Saq). Clagett: "it is exceedingly difficult (if not impossible) to identify the Egyptian constellations exactly with modern depictions, except, of course, for the certain identification of Meskhetyu with the Big Dipper." [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 1367–1420]
- Orion is drawn "reversed" in the Senenmut/Ramesseum tradition (the southern panel is mirror-oriented), which is why he seems to sail east. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT 1458–1460]

**Ramesside star clocks (KV 9, KV 1, KV 6)**
- Two sets in the tomb of Ramesses VI (KV 9), one each in Ramesses VII (KV 1) and Ramesses IX (KV 6); 12th century BC. Each set is 24 tables at half-month intervals (1st and 16th of each month). Each table has the figure of a **seated man** (the "target figure") under a grid of 9 vertical lines (7 inner transit lines; the 5th is the meridian) and 13 horizontal lines (beginning of night, then the ends of the 12 hours). A star marks an hour when it crosses a line: "opposite the heart", "on the right/left eye", "on the right/left ear", "on the right/left shoulder". All sets are textually corrupt; a prototype can be reconstructed (N&P EAT II 1964). [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 749–760; SNIPPET] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ramesside_star_clocks ; https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/10.1086/350100
- Practice: usually reconstructed as two observers seated face to face on the temple roof, one holding the sighting instruments (bay = slotted palm rib; merkhet = plumb line), with a water clock used to set the original hour ends. The transcript's summary: "since it relied on people holding their instruments, a technique like this could not have been very accurate." [DISPUTED in detail] [V-TEXT urania; clagett 757]
- Warning quoted in Clagett (N&P's judgement): "the crudeness of the underlying procedures is so great that only under severely restrictive assumptions could numerical conclusions be abstracted from the given lists … one would do best to avoid all hypothetical structures designed to identify Egyptian constellations from the analysis of the Ramesside star clocks." [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett ~line 760]

**The Book of Nut and the 70 days — the exact linkage**
- Text: the *Fundamentals of the Course of the Stars* (older name "Book of Nut"; correct title recovered by Alexandra von Lieven, *Grundriss des Laufes der Sterne: Das sogenannte Nutbuch*, Carlsberg Papyri 8 / CNI Publications 31, Copenhagen 2007). Witnesses: the ceiling of the Osireion (cenotaph of Seti I at Abydos, c. 1290–1279 BC); the tomb of Ramesses IV (KV 2), the only tomb carrying the running text; and **Papyrus Carlsberg I** (with I a), a 2nd-century AD hieratic copy from Tebtunis with a demotic commentary. Neugebauer & Parker edited the Seti I and Ramesses IV versions and pCarlsberg I in EAT I (1960) as "The Cosmology of Seti I and Ramses IV"; Clagett prints it as Document III.12. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET; V-TEXT clagett line 103] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Nut ; https://books.google.com/books?id=hsKJmVAbv2EC ; https://books.google.com/books/about/Egyptian_Astronomical_Texts_The_early_de.html?id=YDqXI-oAxLIC
- Content (Clagett's summary of Doc. III.12): the decan stars, "on the model of Sirius, disappear in the Duat, i.e., the Otherworld, for 70 days, after which time they rise again. The account is of a simplified scheme of 360 days, an old star dying and a new star being born every 10 days. After spending 70 days invisible in the Duat, a decan rises and spends 80 days in the eastern heaven before it culminates … after which it 'works' for 120 days … Then, its 'work' being over, it spends the remaining 90 days in the western sky before dying once more." (70+80+120+90 = 360.) Sopdet/Sirius is the template and the 35th decan in the list; "the figure of 70 days most likely pertained to the latitude of Memphis or of nearby Heliopolis." [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 669, 722–723; SNIPPET Wikipedia/Book_of_Nut]
- The embalming sentence: the pCarlsberg I commentary, explaining why the stars spend 70 days in the Duat, says (in the translation reproduced on the Book of Nut page; translator not confirmed this session — von Lieven 2007 or N&P 1960): **"He loses his evil for 70 days, that is, after 70 days it is said: 'Therefore they are placed in the embalming workshop for 70 days.'"** [ESTABLISHED that the text makes the link; wording UNVERIFIED against the edition] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Book_of_Nut ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/2404.01458
- How scholars state the linkage (precisely): (1) the *Egyptians themselves* wrote the analogy — the 70-day invisibility of a decan = 70 days in the embalming house (*wabet*), purification, then rebirth on the eastern horizon; (2) Neugebauer & Parker (EAT I, 1960), Clagett (1995) and von Lieven (2007) read the 70-day period of embalming as modelled on Sirius' roughly 70-day conjunction with the sun at Egypt's latitude — not the other way round; (3) the 70 days is independently attested for mummification by Herodotus 2.86 ("covered up in natron for seventy days") and Genesis 50:3 (40 days embalming, 70 days mourning). Nobody in the mainstream literature claims the 70 days encodes anything but this stellar analogy. [ESTABLISHED] https://biblehub.com/genesis/50-3.htm ; sibling file `05_mummification_and_preservation_tech.md` for Herodotus.
- The same text is the one in which Nut swallows the sun each evening and gives birth to it each dawn; the stars "travel outside Nut in the night when they shine … it is within her that they travel in the day when they do not shine" (pCarlsberg I, quoted on Wikipedia). [SNIPPET]

## 3. The civil calendar

**Structure** [ESTABLISHED] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_calendar ; https://www.britannica.com/science/Egyptian-calendar
- 365 days: three seasons — *Akhet* (inundation), *Peret* (growing), *Shemu* (harvest/low water) — of four 30-day months, each month of three 10-day "decades"; plus **five epagomenal days**, "days upon the year" (*heryu renpet*), reckoned outside the months. No leap day, so the year drifted one day every four years against the seasons: the *annus vagus* or "wandering year". [V-TEXT clagett 435–444; urania]
- Neugebauer's verdict (*The Exact Sciences in Antiquity*, 1957), quoted in the transcript: the Egyptian civil year is "the only intelligent calendar which ever existed in human history." [V-TEXT urania] https://archive.org/details/exactsciencesina00neug
- Origin: the earliest Egyptians used a lunar calendar (the month-glyph is a crescent over a star; the month began on the morning the old crescent was no longer visible), intercalating a 13th month by the heliacal rising of Sirius (Parker, *The Calendars of Ancient Egypt*, SAOC 26, 1950). The 365-day civil year was in place by the Old Kingdom; Parker derived 365 either from averaging luni-stellar years or from counting days between Sirius risings. Clagett (1995) rejects Parker's reconstruction of the "old lunar calendar" and his reading of the Ebers calendar as "unproved and indeed untenable", while accepting the later lunar calendar of Papyrus Carlsberg 9. [DISPUTED in detail] [V-TEXT clagett 355, 368] https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/saoc26.pdf
- The five epagomenal days were the birthdays of Osiris, Horus the Elder, Seth, Isis and Nephthys (Plutarch, below) and were regarded as dangerous days requiring apotropaic rites. [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT plutarch; urania]

**Sothic anchors and their disputes**
- **Censorinus** (*De die natali*, AD 238) records that the heliacal rising of Sirius fell on Egyptian New Year's Day (1 Thoth) in the year we compute as **AD 139** (20/21 July, Julian) — the one securely fixed coincidence from which Sothic cycles (~1,460 Julian years) are back-projected. [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sothic_cycle
- **Ebers calendar**: a 12-line table on the verso of the first column of the Ebers medical papyrus (its significance recognised 1870): "Year 9 of Amenhotep I … III Shemu 9: the Going Forth of Sothis", followed by the ninth day of each civil month. Conventional date 1517 BC if observed at Thebes, c. 1537 BC if at Memphis/Heliopolis (Clagett gives the Memphis/Heliopolis limits as 1525–1517 and notes an argument for 1506). Clagett's own theory: the table was made to help build or use a **water clock** with monthly hour scales (see §4). [ESTABLISHED as a document; DISPUTED in interpretation and date] [V-TEXT clagett 519–636, 900] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Amenhotep_I ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ebers_Papyrus
- [FRINGE] a ministry paper puts the Ebers rising at 22 May 1170 BC — 347 years too late for every mainstream chronology. https://www.academia.edu/117233664/
- **Illahun (el-Lahun) papyrus** (P. Berlin 10012): a temple letter *predicting* the rising of Sothis for **IV Peret 16, Year 7** of a king now agreed to be Senusret III, and a receipt on the 17th for the festival offerings. Parker used four lunar dates from the same archive to fix it at 17 July 1872 BC (accession 1878); Rolf Krauss re-dated Senusret's accession to 1837/36 BC (year 7 = 1831/30) by assuming observation at Elephantine; Douglas Keenan argues Sothic dating of the Middle Kingdom is "fallacious" because the observing site and visibility conditions are unknown. Recent work claims the accession years are "confirmed by all twenty-six moon dates … as well as by the Sothic date". [DISPUTED] [V-TEXT clagett 529–541, n. 1649; SNIPPET] https://www.informath.org/SothicEMK.pdf ; https://www.academia.edu/93436170/The_absolute_dating_of_three_pharaohs_of_the_Egyptian_12_th_dynasty ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sothic_cycle
- Last pharaonic Sothic date: a calendar at Aswan, an unknown year of Thutmose III, III Shemu 28 (c. 1464 BC). [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 636]

**The Canopus Decree (238 BC) — the first leap year, refused**
- Issued at Canopus in the western Delta on 7 March 238 BC by the priestly synod under Ptolemy III Euergetes and Berenice II; trilingual (hieroglyphic, demotic, Greek). It ordered a **sixth epagomenal day every fourth year** so that the festivals would stop wandering through the seasons — a 365¼-day year. The priests did not implement it; Clagett calls it "the abortive fixed-year calendar of Ptolemy III". [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 175; SNIPPET] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Decree_of_Canopus ; https://www.instonebrewer.com/TyndaleSites/Egypt/ptolemies/chron/egyptian/chron_eg_anl_canopus.htm
- A complete monolingual stela of the decree was reported found in Egypt in September 2025 (first complete new copy in ~150 years). [ESTABLISHED as news; details SNIPPET] https://archaeologymag.com/2025/09/complete-copy-of-the-canopus-decree/ ; https://www.labrujulaverde.com/en/2025/09/stele-with-a-complete-monolingual-version-of-the-canopus-decree-issued-by-pharaoh-ptolemy-iii-in-238-bc-discovered/
- **Alexandrian calendar**: Augustus imposed the leap day in 25 BC, locking 1 Thoth to 29 August (30 August before a Julian leap year); this reformed year survives as the Coptic calendar (Coptic New Year, Neyrouz, now 11/12 September Gregorian). The old wandering year "remained in use by some astronomers until medieval times" (Ptolemy's *Almagest* uses it). [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT Coptic-calendar mirrors] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Coptic_calendar

**Plutarch on the five days** (Loeb tr. Babbitt 1936, *Isis and Osiris* 12) [ESTABLISHED as Plutarch's text] [V-TEXT plutarch_isis_loeb.xml] https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Isis_and_Osiris*/A.html
- "Hermes … playing at draughts with the moon, … won from her the seventieth part of each of her periods of illumination, and from all the winnings he composed five days, and intercalated them as an addition to the three hundred and sixty days. The Egyptians even now call these five days intercalated and celebrate them as the birthdays of the gods. They relate that on the first of these days Osiris was born, and at the hour of his birth a voice issued forth saying, 'The Lord of All advances to the light.'" (Babbitt's note: 1/70 of 12 × 29 days ≈ 5 days.)

## 4. Clocks and hour-priests

**The Karnak clepsydra (Cairo JE 37525) — oldest surviving water clock** [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 825–947; SNIPPET]
- Found "in broken pieces at Karnak in 1904 by G. Legrain and restored for the Cairo Museum"; reign of Amenhotep III (c. 1391–1353 BC); alabaster (calcite); "about 14 inches high"; an inverted truncated cone, "shaped rather like a flower pot". Museum pages now list it at the National Museum of Egyptian Civilization (NMEC), Cairo — check current location. https://egypt-museum.com/clepsydra-of-karnak/ ; https://aea.physics.mcmaster.ca/index.php/en/?option=com_content&view=article&id=513%3Awco1&catid=137%3Awater-clocks ; https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=3067
- Inside: twelve vertical hour scales, one per month, each divided into 12 hours ("12 columns of 11 false holes" in museum descriptions); scale lengths run from **12 fingerbreadths** (summer-solstice month) to **14** (winter solstice) in steps of ⅓ f; 1 f = 18.75 mm. Clagett: "The 14:12 ratio is not a very good approximation of the length of longest night to shortest night for Egypt. The ratio 14:10 would have been a better one." [V-TEXT clagett 835–845]
- The shortest scale is assigned to civil month II Shemu — correct for Amenhotep I's era, not Amenhotep III's — so the maker "simply copied the monthly assignments of the twelve scales from an earlier clock, and thus the scales were about one month off": out of date when new. [V-TEXT clagett 845–852]
- Outside: three registers — decans with "Isis as Sothis (Sirius) in a bark", planets and triangle decans (top); northern constellations and deities (middle); the king with the twelve month-gods (bottom). A small Thoth-baboon probably sat over the outflow hole, as on later clocks (Horapollo I.16: "in their water clocks the Egyptians carve a seated dog-headed baboon and they make water flow from his member"). [V-TEXT clagett 833, 1729–1736]
- Performance (Sloley, in Clagett): outflow "averaged 10 drops a second"; "the hours were nearly correct at the middle of the scale, the earlier were too long and the later too short … The Egyptians did not know this." Wall slope 70° 25'; Borchardt: it measured the "astronomical night" (twilights excluded). [V-TEXT clagett 853–867, 940–947]
- Recent studies: Schomberg, "The Karnak Clepsydra and its Successors" (BSA 53), https://refubium.fu-berlin.de/bitstream/handle/fub188/23840/bsa_053_12.pdf ; "Votive Gift or Utilitarian Object?", https://www.academia.edu/42228210/The_Karnak_Clepsydra_Votive_Gift_or_Utilitarian_Object [SNIPPET]

**Amenemhet, the man who "invented" the water clock** [ESTABLISHED] [V-TEXT clagett 851–870, 1173; urania]

## 5. The Cairo Calendar of Lucky and Unlucky Days and the Algol claim

- **The papyrus**: P. Cairo JE 86637, published by Abd el-Mohsen Bakir, *The Cairo Calendar No. 86637* (Cairo 1966). Ramesside (19th Dynasty; a passage mentions Ramesses II), dated c. 1244–1163 BC (some give 1271–1163). It gives good/bad prognoses for each day of the civil year with mythological explanations, in the genre Egyptologists call *Tagewählerei* ("day-selection"). [ESTABLISHED] [SNIPPET] https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Cairo-Calendar-No.-86637-Barns-Bakir/a725f64cde8c48a4eb0a91d14b094c699de70d90 ; https://franklin.library.upenn.edu/catalog/FRANKLIN_997831373503681
- Earlier astronomical readings: Christian Leitz (*Tagewählerei*, 1994) first argued the calendar contains astronomical data; Hardy (2003) proposed it was a stellar almanac. [ESTABLISHED as positions] [V-TEXT Berio in CORPUS_INDEX; SNIPPET] https://www.academia.edu/2341091/The_Cairo_Calendar_as_a_Stellar_Almanac
- **The Algol claim** (Lauri Jetsu, Sebastian Porceddu et al., Helsinki): Porceddu et al., *Cambridge Archaeological Journal* 18 (2008); Jetsu et al., *ApJ* (2013); **Jetsu & Porceddu, "Shifting Milestones of Natural Sciences: The Ancient Egyptian Discovery of Algol's Period Confirmed", *PLOS ONE*, 17 Dec 2015**; Porceddu et al., "Algol as Horus in the Cairo Calendar", *Open Astronomy* 27 (2018; arXiv 1810.06412). Method: turn the "lucky" prognoses into time points and test for periodicity (Rayleigh test). Result: a **2.850-day** period (= Algol, whose period today is 2.867 d; the shift explained by mass transfer in the binary) and a **29.6-day** period (the Moon). Claim: the papyrus is "the oldest preserved historical document of naked eye observations of a variable star" and Algol "was represented as Horus and thus signified both divinity and kingship." [DISPUTED] [SNIPPET] https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371/journal.pone.0144140 ; https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4683080/ ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1810.06412 ; https://www.eurekalert.org/news-releases/631384
- **Rebuttals and reservations**: press coverage since 2012 records astrophysicist Peter Eggleton (LLNL): "I think it's an interesting idea — just how convincing it is is another issue." The general objections in circulation are (a) the period is statistical, extracted from a text that never names Algol or any star; (b) the Horus = Algol identification is inferential; (c) Egyptologists explain the prognoses mythologically and calendrically (Leitz's own astronomical reading is itself contested). I could not locate a formal peer-reviewed rebuttal this session; the writers should search Egyptological reviews of Porceddu et al. before quoting one. [DISPUTED; rebuttal citations UNVERIFIED] https://www.space.com/15943-ancient-egyptian-calendar-demon-star.html
- Background for dialogue [BKG]: Algol (β Persei) dips from magnitude ~2.1 to ~3.4 for about ten hours every 2.867 days — visible to the naked eye; the "Demon Star" name is medieval Arabic (*Ra's al-Ghūl*), not Egyptian. https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Algol

## 7. Eclipse and comet records

**The "eclipse" of Takelot II** [DISPUTED]
- The *Chronicle of Prince Osorkon*, carved on the Bubastite Portal at Karnak (ed. Ricardo Caminos, *The Chronicle of Prince Osorkon*, Analecta Orientalia 37, Rome 1958), records that in **Year 15, IV Shemu 25** of Takelot II (22nd Dynasty, 9th century BC) "**the sky swallowed not the moon**" (Caminos' rendering, pp. 58 ff., as cited by J. Leclant in the *UNESCO Courier*, 1963) and that a great civil disturbance broke out in the land. Whether this is a lunar eclipse (the usual Egyptian idiom for one, per Priskin: "at lunar eclipses the sky (Nut) was said to swallow the moon") or a *non*-eclipse used as an omen is the crux; the interpretation, Leclant noted, "has led to a very considerable flow of ink". [V-TEXT inidun/curated_courier 1963; Priskin line 63] https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Chronicle_of_Prince_Osorkon.html?id=IWDaLe8BgH8C ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Takelot_II
- Candidate dates: Kitchen's chronology has long put Takelot II's Year 15 around 850 BC and matched it with a lunar eclipse of 851/850 BC; a recent paper argues "both historical and astronomical methods point towards a lunar eclipse of 15 February 756 BC"; Krauss and others treat the record as ambiguous. The Third Intermediate Period chronology partly hangs on this line. [DISPUTED] [SNIPPET; Kitchen figure BKG] https://www.academia.edu/4105068/The_Lunar_Eclipse_of_Takelot_II_and_the_Chronology_of_the_Libyan_Period ; https://www.semanticscholar.org/paper/The-Chronicle-of-Prince-Osorkon-and-its-Historical-Broekman/239f6679e7bb1acbe7b242af154338965922cf3b

**Solar eclipses: essentially absent** [ESTABLISHED]
- "There seems to be no unambiguous mention of solar eclipses in Ancient Egyptian texts" — despite a sun-centred religion; scholars suspect deliberate avoidance (writing an evil event could make it real), with only oblique references such as "the darkening of the sun". [SNIPPET, from an arXiv preprint and eclipse-history pages] https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.20407 ; https://www.bibalex.org/eclipse2006/historicalobservationsofsolareclipses.htm
- [FRINGE/speculative] a 2024 preprint proposes the total eclipse of 1 April 2471 BC explains the end of the 4th Dynasty's sun-cult (Shepseskaf); a Tutankhamun-era "darkness during daylight" line is cited on eclipse-tour sites. Neither is accepted evidence. https://arxiv.org/pdf/2412.13640 ; https://quakerdave.me.uk/texts/solar%20eclipses%20(dave%20smith)%20-%20part%201.pdf

**The Tempest Stela of Ahmose — the caveat** [DISPUTED]
- A Karnak stela of Ahmose I (founder of the 18th Dynasty, c. 1550 BC) describing a catastrophic storm: days of darkness, rain, noise "louder than the cataract at Elephantine", corpses in the river, temples wrecked, and the king's restoration work. [ESTABLISHED as a text; the quoted phrase is BKG] https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tempest_Stele
- Robert Ritner & Nadine Moeller, "The Ahmose 'Tempest Stela', Thera and Comparative Chronology", *JNES* 73 (2014): a new translation arguing the storm is the meteorological aftermath of the Thera (Santorini) eruption, which would pull Ahmose's reign 30–50 years earlier than the conventional c. 1550 BC. Their line: "If Thera cannot be moved to Ahmose, it is becoming clearer that Ahmose might be moved toward Thera." [SNIPPET] https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/abs/10.1086/675069 ; https://archaeology.org/news/2014/04/04/140404-ahmose-thera-chronology/
- Caveats: (1) Wiener & Allen, "Separate Lives: The Ahmose Tempest Stela and the Theran Eruption", *JNES* 57 (1998), read the storm as literal weather or Hyksos-war metaphor, unconnected to Thera; (2) the eruption date is itself contested — an olive branch buried by the eruption radiocarbons to 1627–1600 BC, but the 1627 BC Greenland sulphate spike has been re-attributed to Alaska's Aniakchak, and Ahmose's mummy dates to a mean of 1557 BC; (3) eclipse and comet readings of the stela are speculative. [DISPUTED] [V-TEXT sovrynn/ecdo mirror of the volcanism timeline; SNIPPET] https://www.biblicalarchaeology.org/daily/news/reinterpreting-the-tempest-stela/

**Comets** [ESTABLISHED negative]
- No Egyptian text is accepted as a comet record. The earliest secure Halley records are Babylonian (164 BC and 87 BC: Stephenson, Yau & Hunger, *Nature* 314, 587, 1985) and Chinese (240 BC); the "1404 BC" Halley apparition is a numerical back-integration (Yeomans & Kiang 1981), not an observation. "Long-haired star" claims for the Pyramid Texts and the Ipuwer papyrus are [FRINGE/UNVERIFIED]. https://www.nature.com/articles/314587a0 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley%27s_Comet ; https://arxiv.org/pdf/1704.00722

## Film hooks (fact → fiction; ⟂ marks where truth ends)

1. **The 70-day timestamp.** FACT: the Book of Nut says a decan star spends 70 days "in the embalming workshop" of the Duat before rising reborn; embalming took 70 days; Sirius is the template. → FICTION: 70 days is the protocol's "handshake interval" — a body is sealed for one full invisibility of Sirius so the star-clock can stamp the burial with an absolute date (decan + civil day + Sothic offset) readable by anyone who can see the sky. ⟂ Truth ends at "analogy": no text treats the 70 days as data storage; scholars read the star-myth as modelling the ritual, not the reverse.
2. **A calendar that only makes sense if you plan to be away 1,460 years.** FACT: no leap day, so New Year drifted through the seasons and met the Sirius rising only every ~1,460 years (AD 139 is the one fixed coincidence); the priests refused the Canopus leap day in 238 BC. → FICTION: the refusal was deliberate — the drift *is* the timestamp; a revived witness reads the offset between civil date and Sirius rising and knows how long he has been "in the Duat". ⟂ The real reason for refusal is unknown (conservatism); Ebers and Illahun show priests recording, not encoding, the drift.
3. **The Dendera disk as a sealed, dated message.** FACT: a 2.5 m stone sky-map whose two eclipses and five planets fix summer 50 BC, cut out with gunpowder and sold to a king; its age fought over by physicists and philologists; Egypt wants it back. → FICTION: the ASI reads the eclipse disks as the "conception date" of the next resurrection; the Louvre gallery is where the cyborg first sees the sky he was buried under. ⟂ Priskin's objection (the planets sit in astrological exaltations) means even the snapshot date is contested; a consensus, not a code.
4. **Algol as the "heartbeat" in a Ramesside calendar.** FACT: a Helsinki team claims a 2.85-day rhythm in the good/bad days of P. Cairo 86637 matches Algol's eclipses 3,000 years ago, and that Algol = Horus. → FICTION: the "lucky days" table is a maintenance schedule keyed to a blinking star. ⟂ No Egyptian text names Algol; the period is a statistical extraction ("interesting … how convincing it is is another issue").
5. **The hour-priests as the machine's last operators.** FACT: hour-watchers sat face to face on temple roofs with a slotted palm rib and a plumb line, calling hours by which star sat "on the right ear" of the man opposite; a water clock built by an official who "read all the books of the divine word" was the backup; Harkhebi boasts he divided the hours "without making mistakes during the night". → FICTION: a human clock-keeping relay kept a schedule alive for 3,000 years until it lost the key. ⟂ The instruments were crude; Neugebauer & Parker warn against extracting precision from the tables.
6. **The king becomes Orion.** FACT: Pyramid Texts 442/466 make the dead king "the companion of Orion" who "navigates the Duat with Osiris", with Sothis as guide; Plutarch says the soul of Horus *is* Orion. → FICTION: the resurrection target is a "return address" written as a star. ⟂ The Giza–Belt ground plan and 10,500 BC are not in the texts (38° vs 47–50°; the inverted map).
7. **A recorded catastrophe of darkness and noise.** FACT: Ahmose's Tempest Stela describes days of darkness and a roar louder than the cataract; one serious 2014 paper ties it to Thera. → FICTION: the stela is the last human eyewitness log of the machines' weather weapon. ⟂ Wiener & Allen read it as weather or metaphor; the Thera date is unsettled; no Egyptian eclipse or comet record exists to lean on.
8. **"The sky swallowed not the moon."** FACT: an Egyptian idiom for a lunar eclipse, used in a 9th-century BC chronicle to date a civil war, still argued over (851 vs 756 BC). → FICTION: the cyborg's first words on waking — something *should* have happened in the sky and did not. ⟂ The phrase's astronomical meaning is genuinely disputed.
