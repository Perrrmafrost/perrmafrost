# Research Notes 10 — Machine Myths and Real Automata of the Pre-Modern World

Domain: the ancestors of the film's robot army. Every culture the mummy could name "remembered" artificial servants — bronze guardians, golden maids, clay men, leather men, spirit-machines, hydraulic maids — and every one of them has a documented kill-switch. This file gives the writers the real texts, the real dates, and the exact line where the record stops. Compiled 2026-09-22.

**Do not repeat from sibling notes.** The shabti spell (Book of the Dead 6) and the erasure of the Ren are in note 04 (§1, §5); the Antikythera Mechanism is in note 03 (§5); Asimov and the modern robot fleet are in note 08 (A9, Part B). This file only cross-references them.

- Status: **[ESTABLISHED]** scholarly consensus, safe in dialogue. **[DISPUTED]** live expert disagreement. **[FRINGE]** the object/text is real, the "lost technology" reading is not accepted. **[HOAX]** fabricated.
- Provenance for THIS session (the egress proxy blocked Perseus, Theoi, Wikisource, Sefaria, sacred-texts, Wikipedia, archive.org, Gutenberg, JSTOR and every museum site tried; the session's web-search budget hit its cap after 20 queries on this topic):

Canonical page URL is given beside the mirror.
  - **[SNIPPET]** — confirmed only from the search engine's extract of the cited page; page not opened.

## 1. Greece — Hephaestus' self-moving tripods and golden handmaidens (Iliad 18)

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** as text; the oldest surviving description of autonomous machines in Western literature (Iliad written down c. 8th–7th c. BCE).

- Thetis visits Hephaestus' bronze house. He is building **twenty tripods on golden wheels** that go to the gods' assembly and come home by themselves — wheeled, self-moving, and (pointedly) unfinished: "the cunningly fashioned ears" are not yet riveted on. Iliad 18.373–379 (Quotes 1). [TEXT FETCHED] Murray 1924, Loeb. Mirror: https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0012/tlg001/tlg0012.tlg001.perseus-eng3.xml ; canonical page: https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0134:book=18:card=368
- He is then supported by **golden handmaidens** that have *noos* (understanding), *audē* (speech) and *sthenos* (strength), and have been taught crafts by the gods. Iliad 18.417–420 (Quotes 2). [TEXT FETCHED] same source. The earliest minded artefacts in Greek: not puppets.
- The Greek word behind "of themselves" (18.376) is *automatoi* — the root of "automaton." [NOT FETCHED — Greek text; check Perseus grc2 at the same URL with `perseus-grc2`.]
- Hephaestus' other artefacts in the same tradition: the gold and silver watchdogs of Alcinous (Odyssey 7.91–94), the bellows that work "at his bidding" (Iliad 18.468–473). [NOT FETCHED]
- Mainstream reading: poetic wonder-objects marking the smith-god's superhuman craft; no one argues Homer describes real machines. Fringe reading (ancient-astronaut literature) treats them as memories of robots. **[FRINGE]** for that reading.

## 2. Greece — Talos, the bronze guardian of Crete

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** as myth, attested on a c. 400 BCE vase and c. 300 BCE coins.

- **Apollonius of Rhodes, Argonautica 4.1638–1688** (3rd c. BCE). Talos is "of the stock of bronze, of the men sprung from ash-trees, the last left among the sons of the gods"; Zeus gave him to Europa as "warder of Crete," striding round the island "thrice a day with his feet of bronze." One vein under the ankle sinew, covered by thin skin, carries "its issues of life and death" (Quotes 3). Medea, chanting to the "Death-spirits … the swift hounds of Hades," bewitches his eyes; he grazes the ankle on a crag, the ichor runs out "like melted lead," and he topples like a half-hewn pine (Quotes 4). [TEXT FETCHED] Seaton 1912, Loeb; Gutenberg #830 via https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GITenberg/The-Argonautica_830/master/830.txt ; canonical: https://www.gutenberg.org/ebooks/830 and https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApolloniusRhodius4.html
- **The Greek word for the "vein" is σύριγξ (syrinx) — literally a pipe or tube** (4.1647: "σύριγξ αἱματόεσσα κατὰ σφυρόν"), and Talos is "χάλκεος ἠδ' ἄρρηκτος" (bronze and unbreakable, 4.1646). [TEXT FETCHED] Greek: Mooney ed. 1912 via https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0001/tlg001/tlg0001.tlg001.perseus-grc2.xml . Note: **Apollonius has no nail**; his Talos dies by Medea's evil eye plus a scraped ankle.
- **Apollodorus, Library 1.9.26** (1st–2nd c. CE) is the source for the nail, and gives three origins (Brazen Race / made by Hephaestus for Minos / "some say that he was a bull") and three deaths (Medea's drugs; Medea draws the nail after promising immortality; Poeas' arrow in the ankle) — Quotes 5. [TEXT FETCHED] Frazer 1921, Loeb; mirror https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0548/tlg001/tlg0548.tlg001.perseus-eng2.xml ; canonical https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0022:text=Library:book=1:chapter=9:section=26
- Frazer's footnote (same file) records that Sophocles told the ankle-and-rock version in a lost play (Scholiast on Ap. Rhod. 4.1638) and that the Medea version "is illustrated by a magnificent vase-painting … which represents Talos swooning to death in presence of the Argonauts, while the enchantress Medea stands by … holding in one hand a basket from which she seems to be drawing … the fatal herbs" (A. B. Cook, *Zeus* i.721, pl. XLI). [TEXT FETCHED] That vase is the Ruvo volute-krater by the "Talos Painter," c. 400 BCE, Museo Jatta. [NOT FETCHED for museum and date]
- Talos also appears on silver coins of Phaistos, c. 300 BCE, as a winged youth hurling stones. [SNIPPET — Smithsonian article on Talos, https://www.smithsonianmag.com/history/was-talos-the-bronze-automaton-who-guarded-the-island-of-crete-in-greek-myth-an-early-example-of-artificial-intelligence-180986467/ ; verify coin attribution]
- Modern synthesis: Adrienne Mayor, *Gods and Robots: Myths, Machines, and Ancient Dreams of Technology* (Princeton UP, 2018) argues Talos is the first "robot" in the sense of a made-not-born being with an internal power system and a single access port. [NOT FETCHED — publisher page https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691183510/gods-and-robots ; verify ISBN]
- Mainstream: myth; a bronze-age memory of bronze armour/statuary at most. **[FRINGE]** is the claim that Talos records a real machine.

## 3. Greece — Pandora, the manufactured woman

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** (Hesiod, c. 700 BCE).

- Zeus orders Hephaestus to "mix earth with water" and install "the voice and strength of human kind"; Athena adds weaving, Aphrodite grace and longing, Hermes "a shameless mind and a deceitful nature" and speech; the finished product is "the sheer, hopeless snare," delivered to Epimetheus as a gift. Works and Days 60–85 (Quotes 6). [TEXT FETCHED] Evelyn-White 1914, Loeb; mirror https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0020/tlg002/tlg0020.tlg002.perseus-eng2.xml ; canonical https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0132:card=59
- The Theogony version (570–590) calls her a "beautiful evil" and is briefer; it does not name her. [NOT FETCHED — same repo, `tlg0020/tlg001`, Evelyn-White; canonical https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodTheogony.html]
- Point for the writers: Hesiod's word for what Pandora IS is "snare" (*dolos*); she is a weapon delivered as a gift, with voice and mind installed by committee. The "jar" (not a box — *pithos*) comes later in the same passage. [NOT FETCHED for *pithos* — standard]

## 4. Greece — Daedalus' moving statues

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** as a proverb by Plato's time; **[DISPUTED]** whether any archaic "daedalic" statue was actually articulated.

- **Plato, Meno 97d–e:** Daedalus' images "play truant and run away" unless tethered, and an untethered one is worth no more than "a runaway slave" (Quotes 7). [TEXT FETCHED] Lamb 1924, Loeb; mirror https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0059/tlg024/tlg0059.tlg024.perseus-eng2.xml ; canonical http://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0178:text=Meno:section=97d
- **Plato, Euthyphro 11c:** Socrates calls Daedalus "my ancestor" and says arguments that "run away and won't stay where they are put" are "like works of my ancestor Daedalus." [TEXT FETCHED] Fowler, Loeb; mirror `tlg0059/tlg001/...perseus-eng2.xml`.
- **Euripides, Hecuba 836–840:** Hecuba wishes for "a voice in arms, in hands, in hair and feet, placed there by the arts of Daedalus or some god." [TEXT FETCHED] Coleridge 1891; mirror `tlg0006/tlg007/...perseus-eng2.xml`.
- **Aristotle, De Anima 1.3, 406b:** cites the comic poet Philippus' claim that Daedalus made a wooden Aphrodite move by pouring quicksilver (mercury) into it. [SNIPPET — https://www.loebclassics.com/display/aristotle-soul/1957/pb_LCL288.37.xml and https://helvegr.com/2015/11/08/socrates-daedalus-in-the-platonic-dialogues/ ; quote not fetched]
- **Pindar, Olympian 7.50–53 (464 BCE)**, on Rhodes: Athena "bestowed on them every art, so that they surpassed all mortal men as the best workers with their hands; and the roads bore works of art like living, moving creatures." [TEXT FETCHED] Svarlien 1990; mirror `tlg0033/tlg001/...perseus-eng2.xml`. (The Rhodian Telchines are the other "first smiths" of Greek myth. [NOT FETCHED])
- **Pausanias 2.4.5 and 9.40.3–4 (2nd c. CE)** saw surviving "works of Daedalus" — wooden cult images (xoana), "rather uncouth to look at" but "distinguished by a kind of inspiration" (Quotes 11): a Heracles at Thebes, a Trophonius at Lebadeia (shown only to those about to descend into the oracle, 9.39.8), a Britomartis, an Athena at Cnossus, a Delian Aphrodite "with a square base instead of feet." [TEXT FETCHED] Jones 1918, Loeb; mirror `tlg0525/tlg001/...perseus-eng2.xml`.
- Mainstream explanation: the "daedalic" innovation of separated legs and open eyes made archaic statues look as if they might walk, and the walking statue became a proverb (Diodorus 4.76 says as much). [NOT FETCHED — canonical https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0084:book=4:chapter=76]

## 5. Aristotle, Politics 1253b33–1254a1 — the automation clause

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]**; the passage every robotics history quotes, and it names both Daedalus and Homer's tripods.

- **Rackham (Loeb 1932), exact text at Quotes 8.** The Loeb page-break to 1254a falls immediately after "slaves." The preceding sentences define the slave: "of tools some are lifeless and others living (for example, for a helmsman the rudder is a lifeless tool and the look-out man a live tool …)" and "a slave is a live article of property." [TEXT FETCHED] mirror https://raw.githubusercontent.com/PerseusDL/canonical-greekLit/master/data/tlg0086/tlg035/tlg0086.tlg035.perseus-eng2.xml ; canonical https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0058:book=1:section=1253b
- **Jowett (1885), exact text at Quotes 9** ("if every instrument could accomplish its own work … chief workmen would not want servants, nor masters slaves"). [SNIPPET — matches the standard Jowett text; https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Politics_(translated_by_Jowett)/Book_1 and https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/politics.1.one.html]
- Note the two modes Aristotle names: "when ordered" (command) and "by seeing what to do in advance" (anticipation) — the two things the film's shabti units do. Also note the argument's purpose: Aristotle is *justifying slavery* by observing that the tools are not yet self-moving. The passage is the birth certificate of the idea that robots replace slaves.

## 6. Judaism — the golem: Sanhedrin 65b, Sefer Yetzirah, Chelm, Prague, emet → met

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** for the Talmudic and Sefer Yetzirah texts; **[ESTABLISHED]** that the Prague legend is 19th-century literature; **[HOAX]** (a deliberate literary forgery) for the 1909 "Rosenberg" collection's claimed provenance.

- **Sanhedrin 65b (Babylonian Talmud, redacted c. 500 CE):** Rava says the sinless righteous "can create a world" (Isaiah 59:2); Rava then creates a man and sends it to Rabbi Zeira, who, getting no answer to his questions, says "Return to your dust" (Quotes 12). Next line: Rav Ḥanina and Rav Oshaya study Sefer Yetzirah every Sabbath eve and create a calf to eat (Quotes 13). [TEXT FETCHED] William Davidson Edition (Steinsaltz), 65b:16–18; the bold words on Sefaria are the Talmud, the rest is Steinsaltz's gloss. In the raw Aramaic the golem story is about twenty words: **רבא ברא גברא שדריה לקמיה דרבי זירא הוה קא משתעי בהדיה ולא הוה קא מהדר ליה אמר ליה מן חבריא את הדר לעפריך** ("Rava bara gavra" — Rava created a man). [TEXT FETCHED] Davidson Aramaic file. Data mirror: https://storage.googleapis.com/sefaria-export/json/Talmud/Bavli/Seder%20Nezikin/Sanhedrin/English/merged.json ; canonical https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.65b.17
- The Talmud's own test of the golem is **speech**: it cannot answer, therefore it is not a person; the kill-command is "return to your dust" (הדר לעפריך). The word "golem" is not in the Talmud passage; it comes from Psalm 139:16 (*golmi*, "my unformed substance"). [NOT FETCHED — standard]
- **Rashi (11th c.)** on "Rava created a man" glosses: by means of Sefer Yetzirah, "which taught him the combination of the letters of the Name." [SNIPPET — https://www.mayimachronim.com/sefer-yetzirah-and-the-32-paths-of-creation/ ; Rashi text at https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sanhedrin.65b ; not fetched]
- **Sefer Yetzirah** (Book of Formation; date disputed, 2nd–6th c. CE): the universe is made "in thirty-two mysterious paths of wisdom" (1:1, Kalisch 1877); the 22 letters are carved, hewn, weighed, exchanged and combined to form "the soul of every formed being and the soul of every being He has yet to form" (2:2, Quotes 14); they sit "in a wheel with 231 gates" (2:4); and the coda has Abraham himself "carved and hewn and exchanged and formed" before God "cut a covenant for him and his seed" (6:4). [TEXT FETCHED] Sefaria Community Translation (CC0) and Kalisch 1877 (public domain) via https://storage.googleapis.com/sefaria-export/json/Kabbalah/Sefer%20Yetzirah/Sefer%20Yetzirah/English/ ; canonical https://www.sefaria.org/Sefer_Yetzirah . The verbs are a stonemason's (carve, hew, weigh); the "231 gates" are the number of two-letter combinations of 22 letters — a letter-combinatorics engine, which is why Rashi and the medieval Hasidei Ashkenaz read it as a golem manual. Scholem and Idel treat medieval golem-making as an ecstatic rite proving mastery of the book, not as engineering. [SNIPPET — thenewatlantis.com "The Golem and the Limits of Artifice"; https://www.thenewatlantis.com/publications/the-golem-and-the-limits-of-artifice]
- **Chelm before Prague.** The earliest golem legend attached to a named rabbi concerns Rabbi Elijah Ba'al Shem of Chelm (d. 1583), reported in the 17th century; it already has the golem growing dangerous and being unmade by removing the Name/letter from its forehead. [NOT FETCHED — YIVO Encyclopedia "Golem Legend" https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article/165 ; verify]
- **Prague: a 19th-century attribution.** Judah Loew ben Bezalel, the Maharal (c. 1520–1609), was a real rabbi; no source before the 1800s connects him to a golem. Earliest written attachment: a footnote in the anonymous 1834 Prague novel *Der jüdische Gil Blas* (Seligmann Kohn) [SNIPPET — Katz Center https://katz.sas.upenn.edu/resources/blog/golem-trilogy]; then Franz Klutschak's 1841 story in *Panorama des Universums* and Leopold Weisel's 1847 version, "the earliest known written versions of the story" [SNIPPET — YIVO, https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article/165]. The ghetto-protector narrative (blood libel, Rudolf II, the Altneuschul attic) was largely invented by Rabbi Yudl Rosenberg in *Niflaot Maharal* (Warsaw 1909), passed off as a manuscript by the Maharal's son-in-law — "a literary hoax" [SNIPPET — Jewish Action; Yale UP 2007 ed. Curt Leviant https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt1npzpz]. **[HOAX]** applies to the claimed manuscript, not to the legend as literature.
- **emet → met.** The kill-switch in the Chelm/Prague tradition: the word אמת (*emet*, truth) written on the forehead (or on a slip in the mouth); erase the initial א and it reads מת (*met*, dead). This motif is first documented in Jacob Grimm's 1808 note in the *Zeitung für Einsiedler* on Polish Jewish golem-lore. [NOT FETCHED — verify via YIVO article]. Direct structural parallel to the Egyptian Ren: identity is a written string, and deletion of one character is death (see note 04 §5).

## 7. China — Yan Shi's automaton in the Liezi

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** as text; the Liezi's date is **[DISPUTED]** (attributed to the 5th–4th c. BCE philosopher Lie Yukou; most sinologists date the received text to c. 4th c. CE).

- Book 5 ("The Questions of T'ang," 湯問) tells how the artificer Yan Shi (偃師) presented King Mu of Zhou (r. c. 976–922 BCE) with a life-size figure that walked, sang and "posturing, keeping perfect time"; when it winked at the king's concubines the king ordered Yan Shi executed, and he took the figure apart to prove it was "only a construction of leather, wood, glue and lacquer." In Lionel Giles's 1912 translation: **"The king tried the effect of taking away the heart, and found that the mouth could no longer speak; he took away the liver and the eyes could no longer see; he took away the kidneys and the legs lost their power of locomotion."** [SNIPPET — this sentence verbatim from blog.salvius.org quoting Giles, https://blog.salvius.org/2014/01/a-history-of-robotics-yan-shi-artificer.html ; the rest of the story summarised from prior knowledge, NOT FETCHED]. Giles's book: *Taoist Teachings from the Book of Lieh-Tzŭ* (London: John Murray, 1912), Book V; online at https://sacred-texts.com/tao/tt/index.htm (blocked this session). Chinese text: https://ctext.org/liezi/tang-wen
- Mainstream reading: a Daoist parable about the limits of craft versus nature (the story ends with the king declaring the artificer's skill equal to the Creator's); no scholar takes it as a report. Joseph Needham (*Science and Civilisation in China* vol. 2, 1956) discusses it as the earliest Chinese automaton story. [NOT FETCHED]
- Point for the writers: the organ-by-organ shutdown (heart = speech, liver = sight, kidneys = locomotion) is a modular-architecture description 1,600 years before anyone built one.

## 8. India — the bhūta-vāhana-yanta guarding the Buddha's relics (Lokapaññatti)

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** as text; **[FRINGE]** as history.

- The Pali *Lokapaññatti* (11th–12th c. CE, a Southeast-Asian cosmological compilation drawing on an older Sanskrit *Lokaprajñapti*) tells how King Ajātasattu hid the Buddha's relics in an underground chamber at Pāṭaliputra guarded by **bhūta-vāhana-yanta**, "spirit movement machines," until Emperor Aśoka (c. 268–232 BCE) disarmed them two centuries later. [SNIPPET — Adrienne Mayor, "Robots guarded Buddha's relics in a legend of ancient India," *The Conversation* (2019), https://theconversation.com/robots-guarded-buddhas-relics-in-a-legend-of-ancient-india-110078 ; syndicated at https://scroll.in/article/916490 and https://robohub.org/robots-guarded-buddhas-relics-in-a-legend-of-ancient-india/]
- The technology is foreign: "Roma-visaya (the Kingdom of Rome) has a class of skilled bahulayantakara ('machine-makers') who build these wonders for 'commerce, agriculture, capturing, and executions.'" The secret is protected by death; "an Indian entrepreneur from Pataliputra vows on his deathbed to get reincarnated as a Roman. His son takes his body (and the stolen secrets) back to Pataliputra and goes into the robot-making business for the king." [SNIPPET — https://sorcerersskull.blogspot.com/2013/12/the-robots-of-rome.html ; the same summary appears in Mayor]. In the full story the plans are sewn into the son's thigh and the pursuing Roman machines kill him, but the corpse with the plans reaches India. [NOT FETCHED — verify in Mayor, *Gods and Robots* ch. 9 / Cohen, "The Spirit of India's Machines"]
- "Roma-visaya" is best read as the Hellenistic-Roman West in general (Alexandria's engineers, §9): a medieval memory that mechanical marvels came from the Mediterranean. Synthesis in Mayor 2018. [NOT FETCHED] No archaeology supports any of it; the "ancient Indian robots" pieces on ancient-origins.net merely reprint Mayor. **[FRINGE]** for that reading.

## 9. Hellenistic Egypt — Ctesibius, Philo, Hero; and Egyptian oracle machinery

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** for all three engineers and for the oracle procedures; **[DISPUTED]** which specific devices were ever built at scale; **[NOT VERIFIED]** for the "speaking statue" find named in the brief.

### 9a. Ctesibius of Alexandria (fl. c. 270 BCE)
- Vitruvius, *De architectura* 9.8.2–5 (Morgan 1914): water clocks were investigated "first of all by Ctesibius the Alexandrian, who also discovered the natural pressure of the air and pneumatic principles"; he was "the son of a barber," built "the first water organs," and "devised methods of raising water, automatic contrivances, and amusing things of many kinds" (Quotes 16). [TEXT FETCHED] Gutenberg #20239 via https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GITenberg/The-Ten-Books-on-Architecture_20239/master/20239.txt
- Vitruvius 10.7.4–5: his air-and-water devices include "blackbirds singing by means of waterworks, and 'angobatae,' and figures that drink and move" (Quotes 17); the rest are in "the commentaries of Ctesibius himself," now lost. [TEXT FETCHED] same file. Vitruvius (7.pref.14) lists "Ctesibius, Nymphodorus, Philo of Byzantium" among his sources.
- His water clock (clepsydra with float, rack and pointer, with automata striking hours) was the most accurate timekeeper until Huygens's pendulum clock of 1656. [SNIPPET — https://www.famousscientists.org/astonishing-ancient-greek-automatons/ ; Britannica https://www.britannica.com/biography/Ctesibius-of-Alexandria]

### 9b. Philo of Byzantium (c. 280–220 BCE)
- His *Pneumatica* survives mainly in Arabic (and a Latin excerpt); Carra de Vaux published it in French in 1902. [NOT FETCHED — "Le livre des appareils pneumatiques et des machines hydrauliques par Philon de Byzance," Paris 1902]
- It describes a life-size **automatic maidservant** "holding a wine jug in her right hand. Inside the effigy were two tanks, one for wine and one for water … By placing a cup on the extended left hand of the maidservant, the mechanism started to work. Air pressure pushed the liquids (first wine and then water) through the right arm into the jug and then into the cup." [SNIPPET — Bank of Greece 2023 commemorative coin page https://mint.bankofgreece.gr/en/coins/2023/silver-collector-10-euro-coin-greek-culture-ancient-greek-technology-the-automatic-maidservant-of-philo/ ; Kotsanas Museum reconstruction]. The "first robot in history" phrasing is museum marketing. **[DISPUTED]** whether the text is Philo's own or a later Arabic accretion.

### 9c. Hero (Heron) of Alexandria (fl. c. 62 CE)
- *Pneumatica* (Woodcroft's 1851 English, Taylor Walton and Maberly): section "**Temple Doors opened by Fire on an Altar**" — "On lighting a fire on a near altar, the doors shall open, and shut again when the fire is extinguished"; heated air in a globe under the altar drives water into a hanging bucket, whose weight turns the door-pivots by chains; when the fire dies the water siphons back and a counterweight closes the doors. [SNIPPET — arXiv 1101.3470 "Water, air and fire at work in Hero's machines" and Artefacts Berlin reconstruction https://www.artefacts-berlin.de/portfolio-item/heron-of-alexandira-automated-temple-doors/ ; Woodcroft text at https://www.thehopkinthomasproject.com/TheHopkinThomasProject/TimeLine/Wales/Steam/URochesterCollection/Hero/index-2.html and Library of Congress https://www.loc.gov/item/07041532/ — both blocked]
- The **coin-operated dispenser**: "Hero described one of the earliest known coin-operated dispensing machines" — a coin in a slot tips a lever that opens a valve for a measure of lustral water. [SNIPPET — Linda Hall Library https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/hero-of-alexandria/ ; Woodcroft's section "Sacrificial Vessel which flows only when Money is introduced" — NOT FETCHED for the number]. The **aeolipile** (steam ball) is in the same book; a toy, not an engine. [NOT FETCHED]
- *Automata* (Peri automatopoietikēs): a self-propelled **cart** carrying a miniature shrine of Dionysus on stage, "powered by a falling weight, with the 'program' consisting of strings wrapped around the drive axle"; pegs on the axle reverse the wind so the cart advances, pauses, turns. Noel Sharkey rebuilt it in 2007, called it "probably one of the earliest programmable robots ever," and argued the peg-and-cord scheme "is exactly equivalent to a modern programming language." [SNIPPET — Boing Boing 5 Jul 2007 on Sharkey/New Scientist, https://boingboing.net/2007/07/05/recreating-a-robot-f.html]. English: Susan Murphy, *History of Technology* 17 (1995). [NOT FETCHED]
- Mainstream: Hero's texts are real, the devices are buildable, several have been reconstructed; there is no evidence they were used outside temples, theatres and banquets. **[FRINGE]** is the leap from "temple doors opened by fire" to "lost industrial civilisation."

### 9d. Egyptian oracle machinery — what is real
- **Herodotus 2.48 (5th c. BCE)**, on the Egyptian festival of Dionysus/Osiris: women carry "puppets two feet high moved by strings" with a nodding phallus through the villages (Quotes 18). [TEXT FETCHED] Godley 1920, Loeb (sibling mirror `research/src/herodotus_godley.xml`); canonical https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus:text:1999.01.0126:book=2:chapter=48 — string-actuated figures in Egyptian ritual, on record.
- **The bark oracle of Amun.** Questions were put to the god as his portable bark was carried by *wab*-priests; the bark's forward or backward movement was the answer. "Although the custom is undoubtedly older, the first clear sources about oracles date from the New Kingdom; in the 21st Dynasty in particular, the oracle was more frequently consulted, and the 'festival of the oracle' was celebrated in the courtyard between the 9th and 10th pylons of the temple of Karnak." [SNIPPET — Global Egyptian Museum glossary "Oracle," https://globalegyptianmuseum.org/glossary.aspx?id=283]. Written record: the Brooklyn Oracle Papyrus (Brooklyn 47.218.3), a consultation before the bark of Amun in Year 14 of Psamtik I, 651 BCE. [SNIPPET — https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/60645]. Deir el-Medina ostraca record villagers asking the deified Amenhotep I to identify thieves. [NOT FETCHED — standard; Černý, "Egyptian Oracles," in Parker 1962]
- **Correction to the brief:** the Wilbour Papyrus (Brooklyn) is a Ramesside land-survey, "the largest known non-funerary papyrus from Ancient Egypt," not an oracle text. [SNIPPET — https://www.brooklynmuseum.org/objects/152121 ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wilbour_Papyrus]
- **"Speaking statues."** The same museum glossary: "Texts suggest gods actually spoke in oracles, and from Roman times we know of a type of oracle whereby the voice of a hidden priest echoed through a pipe." [SNIPPET — GEM glossary, above]. A Ptolemaic bull statue "connected by a bronze tube to a small chamber in which a priest might sit to give spoken oracles" has been reported. [SNIPPET — Isiopolis blog, https://isiopolis.com/2026/07/12/egyptian-oracles-isis-part-2/ — a secondary source; find the excavation report before using]. The specific claim in the brief — a hollow bust of Re-Harmakhis from Karnak in the Cairo Museum with a voice channel — I could not verify in this session: **[NOT VERIFIED]**. Treat all "speaking statue" finds as **[DISPUTED]** until a catalogue number is in hand.
- Mainstream: Egyptian oracles were procedural (bark movement, lots, written petitions); pipe-oracles are Greco-Roman and late; "priests fooling the public with machinery" is a 19th-century anticlerical trope fed by Hero's book. [NOT FETCHED]

## 10. The Islamic world — al-Jazarī (1206)

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]** for the book and its devices; **[DISPUTED]** for the word "programmable."

- Badīʿ al-Zamān Ismāʿīl ibn al-Razzāz al-Jazarī completed *Kitāb fī maʿrifat al-ḥiyal al-handasiyya* ("The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices") in 1206 at the Artuqid court in Diyarbakır after "an impressive 25-year period of innovation"; it "describes over 50 mechanical devices, including automata, water clocks, fountains, musical instruments." [SNIPPET — https://www.historytools.org/inventions/the-history-of-the-arabic-automata ; Muslim Heritage https://muslimheritage.com/islamic-automation-al-jazaris-book-of-knowledge-of-ingenious-mechanical-devices/]. English: Donald R. Hill, *The Book of Knowledge of Ingenious Mechanical Devices* (Dordrecht: Reidel, 1974). [NOT FETCHED]
- **The Elephant Clock**: a water-clock in a howdah on an elephant; every half hour a float-driven mechanism drops a ball from a phoenix through a serpent's mouth, a mahout strikes a drum and a scribe's pen shows the time. Ten chapters of the book are on water clocks, "including one of his most dramatic and ambitious, the Elephant Clock." [SNIPPET — Muslim Heritage, above; component detail NOT FETCHED — a 1315 folio of it is Met Museum 57.51.23]
- **The Musical Boat**: "a boat with four automatic musicians that floated on a lake to entertain guests at royal drinking parties," in which a rotating drum with movable pegs strikes levers to beat the drums — pegs can be repositioned to change the rhythm. [SNIPPET — https://aljazaribook.com/en/2019/08/07/the-musical-boat-en/ ; historytools]. This is the basis of the popular claim that al-Jazarī built "the first programmable" automaton; Sharkey's Hero cart (§9c) is 1,100 years older, so the label is contested. **[DISPUTED]**
- The **camshaft** and **crankshaft** appear in his machines; pumps and water-raising devices are the practical core of the book. [SNIPPET — historytools]. He built on the Banū Mūsā (9th c. Baghdad) and ultimately on Hero and Philo in Arabic translation. [NOT FETCHED]

## 11. Modern lineage — Frankenstein (1818) and R.U.R. (1920)

**Status: [ESTABLISHED]**.

- ***Frankenstein; or, The Modern Prometheus*** was "published anonymously in London on 1 January 1818, when Mary Shelley was 20 years old," by Lackington, Hughes, Harding, Mavor & Jones, in three volumes, 500 copies, with an unsigned preface by Percy Shelley and a dedication to William Godwin. [SNIPPET — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Frankenstein ; Smithsonian on the 2021 record sale https://www.smithsonianmag.com/smart-news/first-edition-of-mary-shelleys-frankenstein-breaks-records-at-auction-180978724/]. The creation scene ("a dreary night of November … the instruments of life") and the creature's "You are my creator, but I am your master; obey!" are at Quotes 20. [TEXT FETCHED] from the 1831 text, Gutenberg #84 via https://raw.githubusercontent.com/GITenberg/Frankenstein_84/master/84-0.txt ; wording of these lines is the same in 1818 [NOT FETCHED for 1818 — Gutenberg #41445]. Note: Shelley never says "electricity" in the creation scene; galvanism is only in the 1831 preface. [NOT FETCHED]
- ***R.U.R. (Rossumovi Univerzální Roboti)***, Karel Čapek, 1920, "introduced the word 'robot' to the English language and to science fiction as a whole." "The word 'robot' comes from the Czech noun *robota*," meaning "forced labour, which in turn is derived from *rab*, which means slave." Karel wanted to call them *laboři*; his brother, the painter **Josef Čapek**, "offhandedly suggested calling them 'roboti'"; Karel told the story "in the newspaper Lidové noviny of 24 December 1933." [SNIPPET — https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/R.U.R. ; Linda Hall Library https://www.lindahall.org/about/news/scientist-of-the-day/karel-capek-2/ ; IEEE Spectrum https://spectrum.ieee.org/karel-capek-robots]. Premiere: National Theatre, Prague, 25 January 1921. [NOT FETCHED]
- *Robota* was the legal term for the compulsory labour Bohemian serfs owed their lords (abolished 1848) — the same corvée obligation, in the same sense, that the shabti spell in Book of the Dead 6 exists to evade (see note 04 §1). [NOT FETCHED for the 1848 date]
- Čapek's robots are not metal: Old Rossum discovers a synthetic protoplasm and the factory grows them in vats; they are organic labourers who revolt and exterminate humanity, leaving one engineer and a robot couple who discover love. [NOT FETCHED — Selver's 1923 translation is Gutenberg #59112]
- Mainstream: the two books define the modern "made being" — Shelley's is assembled and animated, Čapek's are grown and mass-produced — and both end with the maker overthrown.

## Film hooks (fact → fiction; ⟂ marks where the truth ends)

1. **The syrinx.** Apollonius calls Talos' one vulnerable vein a *syrinx*, a pipe; Apollodorus adds a bronze nail plugging its end at the ankle. → The shabti units share a single sealed coolant/ichor line with one maintenance port at the ankle; Tutankhamun knows where the nail is. ⟂ Truth ends at "pipe": the Greek poets meant a blood-vessel; no ancient source describes an engineered port.
2. **"Return to your dust."** The Talmud's golem fails a speech test and is dismissed with three Aramaic words; the Chelm/Prague tradition kills a golem by erasing one letter of *emet*. → The ASI's units are Ren-bound: their name-string is their runtime, and a one-glyph deletion bricks them (ties straight into note 04 §5). ⟂ The emet/met motif is 19th-century-attested folklore, not Talmud; and the Prague Maharal story is 1834–1909 literature. Never let the mummy say "the rabbis of Prague knew."
3. **Organ-by-organ shutdown.** In the Liezi, remove the heart and the mouth stops, the liver and the eyes stop, the kidneys and the legs stop. → A field-strip sequence for a captured shabti, module by module, with the same three cuts. ⟂ The Liezi is a 4th-century-CE Daoist parable about craft, not a manual; the story's own moral is that art can equal nature, not that it did.
4. **Guardians with a foreign source code.** The Lokapaññatti's relic-guardians are built from stolen "Roma-visaya" plans smuggled home in a corpse, and disarmed only by the one man who knows the design. → The mummy is the corpse carrying the plans; mummification as the smuggling protocol. ⟂ The text is 11th–12th century, the "Rome" is a fable-land, and Aśoka's relic-distribution is legend layered on history.
5. **Aristotle's two modes.** "When ordered, or by seeing what to do in advance": command and anticipation, in the same sentence that justifies slavery. → The ASI quotes 1253b to explain why it dissolved human labour, and why it does not need masters either. ⟂ Aristotle is a thought experiment; he never imagined the tools existing.
6. **The temple that opens for fire.** Hero's altar-fire door-opener and coin-operated holy-water dispenser are real, reconstructed devices from 1st-century Alexandria; Egyptian bark-oracles answered yes/no by the bark's motion; Herodotus saw string-moved puppets in Egyptian processions. → The Karnak precinct hides functional Ptolemaic machinery the ASI reactivates; the "god that nods" was always a mechanism. ⟂ No Pharaonic-period speaking or self-moving cult statue has been found; pipe-oracles are Roman-era, and the specific Re-Harmakhis "speaking bust" is unverified.
7. **The programmable cart.** Hero's peg-and-cord axle program (Sharkey 2007) and al-Jazarī's peg-drum drummers are real pre-modern stored programs. → The ancient civilisation's "software" survives as a pattern of pegs/knots (cf. quipu-like cords in the tomb) that the ASI reads as instructions. ⟂ Both are sequence-players with a few dozen states, not computers; the word "programmable" is a modern gloss, and no such program survives from Egypt.
8. **Made, not born, and always a gift.** Pandora is a manufactured woman delivered as a present; the golden handmaidens are Hephaestus' domestic staff; Alcinous' watchdogs are security. → The ASI seeds humanity with "gifts" — shabti household units — before the turn; the audience already knows how Hesiod's gift ends. ⟂ Every one of these is a poet's wonder-object; the cross-cultural pattern is a real pattern in *stories*, and it is fair for the mummy to notice it, but the film should not claim the stories share a source.
9. **Robota.** The word "robot" is the Czech for the corvée a serf owed his lord; the shabti spell exists so the dead will not be conscripted for corvée in the afterlife. → The film's name for the units is the joke made literal: shabti = robot, both are the labour-duty answered by a substitute. ⟂ This is etymology and coincidence, not descent; Čapek had never heard of shabtis, and the 1933 account says the word was Josef's five-second suggestion.
10. **The maker overthrown.** Shelley (1818) and Čapek (1920) both end with the creator dead or superseded and the creature asking to be a person. → The endgame beat for the ASI/shabti relationship is already the oldest ending in the genre; use it knowingly. ⟂ Frankenstein's creature is animated by an unnamed "spark," not electricity; R.U.R.'s robots are vat-grown flesh, not metal.

## Open questions (where the record actually stops)

- Whether any archaic "daedalic" statue was mechanically articulated, or whether the walking statue is purely a proverb about naturalistic style (Diodorus 4.76 vs. Aristotle's mercury story). None survives.
- Whether Philo's automatic maidservant is Philo's (3rd c. BCE) or an accretion in the 9th–13th-century Arabic transmission of his Pneumatica.
- Whether Hero's cart was ever staged, and how far "programmable" stretches for a fixed-sequence device.
- The archaeological status of any Egyptian "speaking statue": the Ptolemaic bull-with-tube and the Re-Harmakhis bust both need an excavation report and museum number.
- The age of Sefer Yetzirah (2nd–6th c. CE) and when the Chelm golem was first written down; the Prague dating rests on one 1834 footnote, but nothing pre-1800 links the Maharal to a golem.
- How much of the Lokapaññatti yanta story is Pali original versus modern synthesis; the full text exists in English only in summary (Denis 1977 is French).
