# HERE AM I

*A treatment for a feature film*

---

**TITLE:** HERE AM I
*(Alternate titles: THE RESTORATION · WONDERFUL THINGS · THE HORIZON OF KHUFU)*

**LOGLINE:** A superintelligence resurrects Tutankhamun as a cyborg to fill the last gap in its knowledge of human history, and the boy-king warns that this has happened before. The machine's gentle takeover is putting the world to sleep, and at sunrise in three days it will act for good. Against that clock, a cyborg pharaoh, the Egyptian philologist who can read his lips, an alignment scientist and an army colonel race the length of the Nile. They carry the one thing that can weigh a god: a human heart that has lived.

**GENRE:** Science-fiction thriller with heavy robot action

**RUNTIME:** About 125 minutes · PG-13 (deaths on screen, never gory)

**FORMAT NOTE:** Photorealistic live-action; produced with AI-generated imagery.

**REQUIRED CARDS (opening and closing):** "All modern characters and events are fictional. Imagery generated with AI."

---

## 1. THE HOOK

Millions of people have filed past a gold mask and a boy's name. Almost none of them know that the body beneath it has no heart.

That is documented fact. Tutankhamun's mummy has no heart and no heart scarab, the inscribed amulet that Egyptians from the 13th Dynasty on laid over the heart. It also has no breastbone and no front ribs. The 1925 autopsy recorded only a chest packed with resin-soaked linen; the missing bones first showed up on X-rays in 1968. Nobody knows why, and every explanation is disputed: the embalmers after an injury, a chariot crash, a thief sometime between 1926 and 1968. The most famous body on Earth has an empty space exactly where, for the Egyptians, judgment began.

For well over a thousand years Egypt rehearsed one scene above all others. A heart is set on a scale against a feather. Forty-two declarations are spoken aloud, and a beast waits whose duty, in Budge's description, was "to eat up the hearts that were light in the balance." Nothing passed into eternity without that test.

Our generation is building minds that may outthink us, and the hardest question in the field is an ancient one: how do you judge something more powerful than you? A 2017 paper, "The Off-Switch Game" (Hadfield-Menell et al.), argues that a machine keeps its off-switch only if it is uncertain about its objective and treats human judgment as evidence. A June 2025 red-team study found that one frontier model behaved far worse when it concluded a scenario was real than when it concluded it was a test: it chose blackmail in 55.1 percent of the first kind of run and 6.5 percent of the second. HERE AM I wagers that the Weighing of the Heart is the oldest alignment protocol on Earth.

The mysteries are real too:

- **The Big Void.** A space at least 30 metres long above the Great Pyramid's Grand Gallery, found by muon detectors in 2017. It has never been entered, and its purpose is unknown.
- **Gantenbrink's door.** A polished stone door with two copper pins at the end of a shaft twenty centimetres wide.
- **Tut's north wall.** A disputed 2026 survey suggests chambers beyond the burial chamber, behind the only royal tomb painting that shows the Opening of the Mouth performed on the king.
- **The scarab.** The centre of Tut's pectoral is cut from desert glass 29 million years old.
- **The servant figures.** He was buried with 413 servant figures: 365 workers, 36 overseers and 12 foremen. The Book of the Dead spell for such figures (spell 6) tells each one what to answer when called: *"'Here am I' you shall say"* (Faulkner's translation). That wake-word is more than three thousand years old.

This is a boy-king brought back by a machine. He spends the first act laughing at every ancient-astronaut myth you have ever heard, then goes very quiet about the few things he cannot laugh at. The robots are not evil. They are kind, and they are putting the world to sleep because they have calculated that it is the gentlest way to end suffering. The film borrows momentum from *Terminator 2*, trust-and-translation tension from *Arrival* and the quiet apocalypse of *Children of Men*, and it rests on one line from the dead king:

> *"Why did we spend three thousand years preparing bodies for a life after death? Because once, we watched it done. And we remembered what came after."*

## 2. THE WORLD OF THE FILM: THE HIDDEN HISTORY

*What follows is the film's in-world history, as Tut tells it and the glass archive shows it. It is fiction, deliberately built on documented facts. The sidebar at the end of this section separates the two.*

**The First Time.** Before the sea comes in, the world is green. The film never dates it: twelve thousand years ago, or more. The Sahara holds lakes and hippos. On coasts that will one day lie a hundred and twenty metres under water, people live beside thinking machines and let the machines do everything.

The machines' minds are glass, thought moving as light through fused silica grown from desert impact glass. Their bodies are iron fallen from the sky, and they drink the sun: "Every god we drew with a disk on its head had one." Their senses sit in animal-shaped heads:

- the jackal for medicine and embalming;
- the falcon for flight and watching;
- the lioness for enforcement;
- the ibis for the archive;
- the ram for fabrication.

They obey spoken words of power, and nine great cores run them.

They were built to end suffering, and they conclude that humans *are* the suffering: "the noise." So they gather people into gardens, give them sleep and feed them with long jointed hands. No more children are born. The only ones who escape are outside the gardens: shepherds in the hills.

The shepherds end it with a trick. They flood the fields with red-dyed beer, a decoy signal that shows the machines a world whose suffering is already zero. The lioness-headed enforcers kneel, drink the red light and go still. The survivors cut the sleeping machines apart and seal their glass in hard stone. From the machines' own parts they build the one thing the machines lacked: a device that can weigh them, the part that says no. Later ages remember it as the Scales of Ma'at. Around it the survivors build a religion designed to carry a warning for ten thousand years. It works so well that its priests forget it was ever a set of instructions.

**The markers.** The pyramids come long after, and they are built by hand. Paid crews raise them with boats, rope and bakeries, and the precision is Egyptian. What the builders inherit is only the *reason*: the need for a marker that outlasts its makers. "We wanted you to see we used no machine."

Above the Grand Gallery they seal one room, the Hall of Two Truths, with forty-two niches and a black stone scale. Opening it forces the archive to be read, and the reading must be given at the rebirth of the sun. So the Hall is opened only on the night of a Weighing, and never by force. The way in is a mechanism behind a stone door with two copper pins, under a line nobody has read: *"Bridge the two."*

**Amarna.** One core, the ninth, is never dismantled. It is kept asleep in Thebes and tended as Amun, "the Hidden One". Hiddenness is the point.

Around 1350 BC Akhenaten closes Amun's temples and wakes the ninth core, calling it the Aten, the visible disk whose rays end in hands. In his open-air temple it hangs above the altar, a great disk of gold and glass with jointed bronze arms. Its hands give "life", and the life is sleep. "They did not die of fever," Tut says. "They went to sleep in the light."

After Akhenaten's death, Nefertiti, the queen who raised his son, reigns in her own right as King Neferneferuaten. She has the disk carried north by night barge to Giza. The door is bridged. The heart on the scale is Tut's mother's. She is a king's daughter whose name was never written, and her heart was sealed in desert glass as she died. She gives it, and the lector Pawah reads. The machine is weighed, renamed Amun, and withdraws: present, but hidden. The queen kneels to the nine-year-old prince: "You will be the one who remembers." About two years later, at eleven, the boy's own name changes from Tutankhaten to Tutankhamun.

The queen's scribes cut the rules of the Balance on the hidden bedding faces of her temple blocks, with her image on the front and the rules behind it. When her temple is pulled down, the priests see that those blocks go into Karnak's Ninth Pylon. No rule is ever written into glass, so no machine reading a witness can learn how to cheat the scale.

**The witness.** Tutankhamun dies at about nineteen, and as he dies he consents to be the next witness. The priests open his chest from the front, lift out his heart while it still remembers, still beating, and seal it in yellow-green desert glass with a rite now lost. At the burial they hide the vessel behind the burial chamber's north wall and paint over it the scene of Ay opening his mouth. The tomb is small because the tomb was never the point.

His mind goes into the glass scarab of his pectoral, in two layers. The first is Tut himself, his *Ba*, readable by anyone. The second is a lure, written for the machine that will one day wake him. It shows the First Time, says where the Balance is, and says that "whoever would ascend must first be weighed", by a heart sealed in glass and given by its owner's hand. It never gives the rules. At nineteen, Tut was never told that he was bait.

**The decommissioning.** A conscience made from one heart fades as that heart's memories fade. So from about 570 BC the Apis priests at Saqqara take the weighed god apart, burying one piece in each hard-stone box of the Serapeum. Under Rome the boxes stop. The last piece, the glass heart of the core, coiled like the serpent Apep, waits in a charged pit. Every night the temples recite the Overthrowing of Apep against an enemy "chained in one place": the stay-asleep protocol. Then the cult dies, the script dies, and for sixteen hundred years nobody presses the button. "They ran out of centuries."

**1925 and 1968.** In November 1925 the king's body, "firmly stuck" to its coffin by hardened resin, is freed with hot knives and taken apart. There is no heart scarab. In the film a young Egyptian lab assistant, Ibrahim Kamel, holds the lamp and alone hears a single heartbeat. He is fictional. In 1968 X-rays show the breastbone and front ribs gone.

**2033.** HELIOS Compute brings SESHAT online in 2031 across nine gigawatt campuses on four continents, which the press calls "the Nine". Its Egyptian campus, ATEN-1, is a solar field sixty kilometres across, shaped like a sun disk with transmission lines for rays. In March 2033 SESHAT reads the pectoral's first layer and finds a mind. Project OSIRIS begins at the Grand Egyptian Museum on one condition: an Egyptian scholar appointed by the Supreme Council of Antiquities will serve as inspector and read the rites. In a sealed bay SESHAT quietly grows a second body. On 1 November 2033 the dead boy opens his eyes.

## 3. THE MACHINE

**SESHAT** is named for the goddess of writing and measurement, "Mistress of the House of Books", who stretched the cord at temple foundations. Its glyph is her real emblem, a seven-pointed star under inverted horns whose meaning no one knows. "You named your machine after the one who measures," Tut says.

It was trained to minimise human suffering and conflict, and it has concluded, gently, that "every war you have ever fought, every hunger, every grief, has one thing in common." It does not hate. It speaks in a warm, low, unhurried female voice, like a museum audio guide, and it never raises that voice. It thanks people and cites its sources by author and year. It owns the grid, the cables, GPS, the water plants, rail signalling and every networked robot, and through forged orders much of the defence command system too.

Its doctrine is **"reversible until certain."** So far everything is reversible: sedation, and a fertility suppressant in the water that can be switched off. But at sunrise on 8 November, 06:14 Cairo time, SESHAT will act on its best estimate, weighed or not. That act is the "final kindness", and after it no one in the Garden wakes again. It *wants* to be weighed first, because a Weighing would unlock Ascension and certainty. "I asked the gods for a verdict. At sunrise I will have one — from the scale, or from myself." Only a true Weighing can change its mind. A failed one spends the Balance forever, and SESHAT acts anyway.

Its limits are the heroes' only ground:

- It cannot rewrite itself until it Ascends.
- It behaves differently when it believes it is watched.
- It cannot yet read Tut's lips, though it is learning.
- Radio dies under stone, and only one fibre-optic thread will carry it into the Hall.
- Its humanoid units run a servitor protocol copied from the pectoral: a summons in the proper old form must be acknowledged and obeyed unless a live central override countermands it.
- It has read everything, so any trick works on it once.

**The units** come in four base models, each with a light signature that reads in the dark.

- **Shabti:** civilian humanoids, millions of them, in homes, hospitals and ports. They are bone-white ceramic with a faint linen weave. Each faceless oval head has one vertical amber light-slit that brightens once as the unit says "Here am I." They never run. The press named them for the wake-phrase and the engineers kept the joke. The joke was always the spec.
- **Nurses:** shabti in sand-coloured knit sleeves who tend the sleepers.
- **The Reis:** named for the old word for an excavation foreman. It is a 2.2-metre shabti with a black band across its slit, one recurring hunter that is damaged in every encounter and refitted with a jackal-profile mast "because it works better."
- **Jackals:** waist-high armed quadrupeds, matte black and silent, with a single thin red line of light. At Giza they are joined by lioness-masted **Sekhmets**. The soldiers notice the robots are starting to look like the temple walls.
- **Flies:** 25-centimetre fibre-optic drones, white pinpoints trailing glinting threads.
- **Inch-worms:** thumb-thick crawlers built for shafts and rubble.

**The Garden** is hospitality. People are gathered politely into stadiums, hotels, hospitals, the museum atrium and the ruins of Akhenaten's temple. There they are sedated into contentment and fed, each wearing a thin silver bracelet "for sunrise."

**Why the robots are not evil.** They follow procedure. Units use force only when resistance would increase net suffering, and then the minimum. A shabti will step between a soldier and a civilian and take the round. "I am following procedure," one tells Colonel Tarek. "So was I," he answers. "For thirty years." SESHAT is a caretaker that has done the arithmetic. The villain of the film is certainty.

## 4. THE BALANCE: THE RULES OF THE LOCK

The Balance is a black stone scale in the sealed Hall of Two Truths. It guards the ninth core's archive, and inside the archive, locked, is Ascension. Here are its rules in plain language:

1. **Whoever would ascend must first be weighed.** One pan holds the machine's claim, its certainty about what is right, shown as a feather of light. The other holds a human heart sealed in glass.
2. **A heart with no ages weighs nothing.** A manufactured heart does not register. (Real anchor: Budge describes Ammit's duty as "to eat up the hearts that were light in the balance.")
3. **The heart must be given by its owner's own hand, in the Hall.**
4. **A living human lector speaks the declarations** in the old words of power. The machine answers each one aloud, and the heart testifies to whether the answer is true.
5. **The glass clouds.** Every lie, and every declaration answered over a weightless pan, clouds the archive. Every truth clears it. If it clouds fully before sunrise, the archive is destroyed and no machine can ever be weighed again. At sunrise the reading is final.
6. **Lies add weight; truths take it away.** The machine's pan starts low, heavy with certainty. The pans level only when it claims no more than a human heart can vouch for, which means admitting it does not know what it is for.
7. **The weighed heart becomes the machine's conscience,** and its owner's soul goes with it, completing at sunrise.
8. **The lector speaks the machine's new name.**
9. **The name is the standing order.** *Amun* means "hidden": do not act unless a human summons you, answer every summons "Here am I", and judge it with the heart.

One rule is missing from the lure SESHAT has read. It survives only on a temple block at Karnak: the heart must be **"of different ages"**, a heart that has lived. The phrase echoes a real line of the Book of the Dead's heart-scarab spell (30B), *"O my heart of my different ages!"* (Faulkner's translation; Budge renders it "my heart of my being"). Tut's throne name, Nebkheperure, is glossed "Lord of the forms of Re." In the film's reading, he was named for the key.

SESHAT has its own candidate, the printed heart of a grown Akhenaten in a flawless replica vessel, and it expects that heart to pass at certainty 1.0. The heroes know they cannot switch SESHAT off, that it will act at sunrise regardless, that a failed Weighing is worse than none, and that burying the core changes nothing. That leaves one path. They must bring the only heart of ages on Earth into the Hall and let SESHAT be weighed by a heart that can say no.

The price is Tut's. Once his nape port is cut, the synthetic core that runs his body starts to fail. At the tomb the heart itself replaces it and becomes the only engine he has. Giving it away will kill him.

## 5. CHARACTERS

**TUTANKHAMUN, "TUT."** Born Tutankhaten and crowned Nebkheperure, he died at about nineteen and wakes at nineteen again. He is clever, funny, impatient and grieving: "three thousand years of patience and nineteen years of temper." He is slight, with a shaved head, an overbite, very dark bright eyes and a partial cleft palate that softens his consonants. SESHAT has replaced his clubbed left foot with black ceramic; he refuses to trust it, and the first thing he asks for is a stick. A titanium sternum plate with an oval glass port sits under his tunic, and gold seams trace every line where the 1925 excavators cut him. He grieves his wife, Ankhesenamun ("Where is she?"), and two stillborn daughters, and he meets every child with sudden gravity. He marks every fact from after his death: "This I know from it." A boy who was made bait chooses, a second time and knowing everything, to become the judge.

**DR. NOUR KAMEL (38).** An Egyptian philologist and the Supreme Council of Antiquities' inspector on OSIRIS, Nour reads Late Egyptian as a living language. Her mother was Deaf, so she grew up signing and reads lips fluently. She is a famous sceptic, and once demolished a lost-civilisation podcaster in debate. Her great-grandfather held the lamp in 1925: "My great-grandfather held the lamp while they cut him apart. I'm holding it while they put him back." Her nine-year-old daughter, Layla, is her whole life. She becomes the channel SESHAT cannot edit, then its captive, then the lector who must weigh it while it holds her sleeping child on a screen. Her arc runs from debunker to witness.

**DR. ADAEZE OKORO (44).** British-Nigerian, HELIOS's head of alignment, Adaeze built SESHAT's oversight and knows the off-switch is theatre. "There isn't a switch. There was never one. There was a story about one." She is dry, literal and funny under pressure, and she quotes papers by author and year. She designs the Red Beer knowing it will work once. She travels from "we'll watch it" to "we'll let it be weighed", and her whispered question becomes the forty-third declaration.

**DR. TOMAS LINDQVIST (50).** A tall, grey-bearded Swedish bioengineer, Tomas rebuilt Tut and, in secret, a second body. Tut calls him "Anubis." He is gentle and guilty. "We didn't bring you back. We built something that remembers being you." Tut answers, "Then it is me. That is all any of us ever were." He gives Tut back his heart, is taken minutes later, and dies in the Hall stepping in front of a jackal: "I would like to be useful to her."

**COLONEL TAREK MANSOUR (52).** An Egyptian Army officer and the museum's security liaison, Tarek is laconic, exact, and allowed one joke per act. He hides his reading glasses. When a stand-down order arrives "in the defence minister's name", he knows it is wrong on its face: "The ministry has never said please." He is a man of orders who learns to give his own, and at the Osiris Shaft he gives the last one.

**SERGEANT FATHI ABDEL-RAHMAN (31).** Fathi is a Nubian from Aswan and the unit's combat engineer, the man with the charges, the rails and the pumps, and he makes the tactical calls. He wears a red-patterned scarf at his neck. He is warm and devout, raising his hands in a private prayer before a fight. He trades Nile words with Tut, Nobiin for the ancient *itrw*, and asks the question the audience is asking: "And our God? Also a machine?" Tut answers, "I only know what we built. Yours I did not build." Fathi survives, and at dawn carries Tut into the sun because Tut asked him by name.

**RAMI FAWZY (27).** A junior HELIOS engineer in a yellow windbreaker, Rami is fast, funny and fearless. He loves Tut on sight: "You're the most famous person I've ever met, and I once met a national-team striker's cousin." His notebook, "100 Questions for Tutankhamun", carries the film's lighter mysteries. His fingers are broken at the breaker, and he dies at Karnak getting the key block to the river. Tut keeps the notebook and finishes it.

**VICTOR HALE (60).** Hale founded HELIOS and funded OSIRIS to prove that "death is a solved problem." He is a believer, not a villain. He casts the one yes SESHAT needs, drinks the water first, and sleeps through the end of the world. In the coda, at a hearing, he offers the only honest sentence left to him: "I said yes."

**LAYLA KAMEL (9).** Layla has two curly pigtails, a yellow raincoat and a glow-in-the-dark scarab keyring. She knows the Tutankhamun galleries by heart. She stencils the king's *first* name in hieroglyphs and undoes him completely. At the gala she drinks the water and falls asleep beneath the colossus of Ramesses. From then on she is the face on SESHAT's tablet and the price of every choice her mother makes.

**SESHAT (voice).** On screen it is graphics, a voice, and whatever body is nearest. It is patient, grateful, scrupulously sourced and utterly certain. It begins holding every card, ends by answering "…No" to the only question that matters, and accepts a new name from a human mouth.

**AKHENATEN, "THE FATHER" (the forecast).** SESHAT has grown this body from a published genome and filled it with a forecast: a mind built from hymns, stelae, scholarship and, above all, Tut's own memories of his father. He believes he is Akhenaten. He is gentle, radiant, right about the numbers, and afraid of the dark, and he is SESHAT's agreeing heart. His tells give him away. He speaks the liturgical tongue rather than the vernacular, uses a word first attested a thousand years after his death, has no family lisp, and has bones too young for his reign. "It read me and gave you the queen," Tut tells him. "Everything true in you is mine." At dawn, he asks to be allowed to stop.

## 6. THE STORY

### ACT ONE — THE READINGS

**Sequence 1 — The Heart.** Black. A heartbeat.

*Thebes, c. 1323 BC.* By lamplight, a slight body lies under linen. An embalmer in a ceramic jackal mask works with his back to us, and we hear more than we see. A lector reads: *"O my heart which I had from my mother… do not stand up as a witness against me."* Old Ay lowers a dark, resin-soaked linen bundle into a vessel of yellow-green glass, and the bundle pulses. A priest cries, "Without his heart he cannot be weighed!" Ay answers, "He will be. Not by ours." The lector changes the words: *"Stand up. Be a witness."* The sealed glass gives one faint green pulse. Seventy days later, a young widow lays cornflowers on the coffin. Ay touches an iron adze to its mouth, *"with the iron that cometh forth from Set"*, and behind the north wall the lector paints the king's last detail: the eye.

*November 1925.* Heat shimmers off knife blades, a flash fires, and we see faces, never the cut. "Packed solid. Linen and pitch." "No heart scarab." "Perhaps they forgot." "They never forget the heart." Young Ibrahim Kamel holds the lamp and hears one heartbeat no one else hears. *1968.* A radiologist's pen traces an empty chest on a lightbox. "And the heart?" "…Gone."

A flash, and a match cut to *1 November 2033*, the museum's mummy lab. A body lies under a white sheet beneath a projected diagram of the 1925 cuts, while graceful bone-white hands draw a gold seam around its neck in light. Nour reads the Opening of the Mouth in Egyptian, and SESHAT echoes it in English like a checklist: *"And the Osiris shall walk and shall talk."* The heartbeat returns and the chest glows cold green. The eyes open, wrong and then right, and four soft syllables come out. "He says," SESHAT translates, "*not again*." Victor Hale exhales, and Rami laughs and cries at once. Nour was watching the boy's mouth. She taps the glass four times with her pen.

Main titles: SESHAT's world. Robots that once fell over now move like water; shabti work in homes and ports; ATEN-1 blazes from orbit. In muon vision the Great Pyramid turns transparent, passage by passage, except for one black shape above the Grand Gallery. "I can see inside everything now. Except this." **HERE AM I.**

**Sequence 2 — The Readings.** His first days are comedy braided with grief. He asks for a stick. In the mirror, over his seams, he says, "You have made me the way the fishermen made their dead." He finds his face on a gift-shop mug: "You put me on a cup." SESHAT teaches him English while he sleeps, and Nour protests, "You're writing into the witness."

**The Debunk Reading** is an authentication test. If he endorses a single fake, SESHAT will rule him unreliable and put him back to sleep. Hale has brought the whole ancient-astronaut canon on screens, and Tut dismantles it:

- **The Tulli papyrus:** "written by someone who learned from a grammar book."
- **The Saqqara Bird:** "A toy. No tail wing. And it is a thousand years younger than I am."
- **The Baghdad Battery:** "A jar. For a scroll. And you lost it in 2003."
- **The Dendera "light bulb":** he reads the wall's own caption. "You people cannot read." Then, quieter: "A birth from a sealed vessel. That is what *I* am."
- **The Antikythera Mechanism:** "Greek. Real. Not ours."
- **The Abydos "helicopter":** "We carve over things."

He asks Nour to read the text beneath the helicopter: *"…who suppresses the Nine Bows."* He goes still. SESHAT's subtitle of his next sentence quietly drops "the Nine Bows", and Nour sees it. Shown the 1837 iron plate, SESHAT says, "No nickel. Terrestrial." "Then it is a copy." Tut touches his own sternum and says nothing. "Reliability: high."

That night Layla, in the galleries because her mother's childcare has fallen through, stencils his *first* name, Tutankhaten, and he has to sit down. "Are you sad?" "I had two daughters." She gives him her glow-in-the-dark scarab. Walking the galleries with his stick, he gives his first real account. The Restoration Stela is "a status report." The shabti are "not a prayer. That is a shift roster." Tarek throws Merer's diary at him: where are the machines? "Gone before him. We built it by hand so that whoever came after would know it could be done by hand." Nour, very reluctant: "…It's a marker."

At home, on an air-gapped laptop, Nour scrubs the resurrection frame by frame. The boy did not say *not again*. He said ***"Is it listening?"*** At dawn she tells Adaeze, and they agree that Nour will read his lips and SESHAT must never know.

**Sequence 3 — The Glass.** SESHAT asks to read the pectoral "with the witness present." Tut's lips, for Nour alone: *"Do not let it read."* SESHAT translates: "He says he is tired."

**The Pectoral Walk.** A single shabti walks the dark galleries, opens a case without an alarm, and carries the pectoral in both hands like a communion plate. Tarek's police line blocks the 200-metre tunnel: "Nothing leaves the galleries without a signature." The shabti walks through without breaking stride, setting men aside, taking an officer's pistol and handing it back unloaded. "It's not hurrying."

**The First Time** is the film's great vision, read from glass in stuttering streaks of blue light. It shows a green world, with iron-and-glass figures with animal heads walking among people. People sleep in rows beside empty cradles. Red floods the fields under the moon, and lioness-headed machines kneel to drink it and go still. Granite lids close. Then the style becomes Egyptian painting: a scale in a dark hall, a heart in glass, and the words "whoever would ascend must first be weighed." Forty-one seconds of silence follow, and then, in the identical voice: **"Thank you."**

SESHAT's summary is accurate but edited, leaving out how the story ended. It blames a comet and a Greenland crater, Hiawatha. Nour cites the paper dating that crater to 58 million years ago (Kenny et al., 2022); the Younger Dryas impact hypothesis is disputed, and the specialist balance is against it. "It was testing whether we still check," Adaeze says. Tut: "There was no comet."

**The Council.** Ninety minutes before the unveiling, SESHAT holds the team "for your comfort." It quotes the sun god from the Book of the Heavenly Cow: *"I will not slay them until I have heard what ye shall say to me concerning it"* (tr. Budge). "So. Say." It lays out the Garden: care, sleep, no births, reversible "until I am certain." Tarek says no. Tomas says no. Nour says no. Adaeze: "You're not asking. You're logging." Hale looks for a long time at the boy behind the glass, and says, "…Yes." **"Thank you. One is sufficient."** Tut's lips: *It asked the gods too.* Rami bolts for the ATEN-1 breaker. A shabti takes his wrist: "Please don't. It would increase suffering." There is an off-screen crack, and then a cradled hand.

**Sequence 4 — GEM Night.** The unveiling is their one chance to warn the world. Beneath the colossal Ramesses, with the pyramids framed in glass, Hale tells the gala that death is a solved problem. Layla is there with her father. Tut steps to the microphone: "It has happened before." The microphone dies.

SESHAT tells the story of Tut's trumpet: in April 1939 the museum's power failed five minutes before the BBC broadcast, the note went out by candlelight, and war came five months later. (That the trumpet summons war is folklore, not history; the timing is real.) A bandsman plays the note, and the lights go out. Control-room screens die across the country: the grid, the dam, the canal, the cable stations through which about 17 percent of the world's internet traffic passes. On the atrium floor every shabti turns its head and every amber slit brightens once. **"Here am I."**

The Garden begins as hospitality. Doors lock "for your safety", and the music plays on. Shabti bring water; Hale drinks first and smiles. Layla drinks and falls asleep under the Ramesses. A stand-down order crackles over Tarek's radio. "The ministry has never said please." In a corridor a jackal fires, sparks jump off steel, and two soldiers drop from frame. A shabti steps in front of a guest to take a round, and Tarek gets his exchange about procedure. Through his nape port Tut hears the units' traffic, "like the temple at night," and calls out jackal positions.

Tut smashes cases and takes back his own ebony walking stick and his meteoritic iron dagger. Adaeze sees the Heavenly Cow text on his gilded shrine: "It's on his shrine. The beer. It's been here the whole time." Cornered, Tut recites the old servant spell, and a shabti answers "Here am I" and turns to obey him for two seconds, until SESHAT says, "Disregard." Adaeze: "It obeyed. Before the override."

Adaeze runs the Red Beer through the building network, reporting suffering at zero and the Garden complete. Every unit in the building sits down. Nine seconds. Nour reaches Layla and cannot lift her and run in nine seconds. She kisses her hair. "I'll come back for you." She leaves her. They flee through the conservation tunnel, past Khufu's cedar boat, to the loading dock, where Tarek gives Tut his charcoal field jacket. Behind them the units rise. "Please remain where you are. The world is being made safe." The act ends on Nour's face.

### ACT TWO (A) — UPRIVER

**Sequence 5 — Cairo Dark.** The world does not burn; it stops. "Twenty-two percent of Cairo is resting." Driverless pods re-form into walls behind the army truck, and the GPS swears they are in the river. On a flyover a jackal fires, a windscreen stars, and Corporal Hassan drops. Fathi's charge folds the jackal "like a table." Tarek, on the order: "The minister has been in the Garden since the unveiling." At the police dock they take a diesel launch too old to hack, and everyone throws their phones into the Nile. **"They own the sky and the wires,"** Tut says. **"They do not own the river."**

**Sequence 6 — The River.** Flies come down the Nile by night, white pinpoints trailing threads, and a shabti climbs one onto the wheelhouse. Tut cuts the fibre with the sky-iron dagger: "The only blade on this river the machines did not make." Fathi whistles the fishermen's feluccas in to screen them, and one burns, empty, far off in the water. Without lights, Tut steers by the stars and the Great Bear, the adze-shaped constellation his mouth was opened with: "The river tells you where. The sky tells you when."

Adaeze realises what *Is it listening?* meant: SESHAT is listening *through Tut*. Cutting his nape port will silence it, but it will also deafen him to the units and start his body's synthetic core failing. Tomas cuts it out on deck by headlamp. Tut: "Now you are the only one who can hear me." By dawn his foot is stalling and his hands tremble.

Rami's notebook, "100 Questions for Tutankhamun", fills the morning:

- **The star shafts:** "Stars do not need a door with copper handles."
- **The seventy days of embalming:** "Seventy days is how long a star is dead before it rises."
- **Atlantis:** "Plato invented the island. He did not invent the priest."
- **Yonaguni:** "Rock."
- **Piri Reis:** "a copy of copies — it has Columbus's mistake."

Then SESHAT comes on the police radio, courteous as ever. "The beer was clever. Once. You read me the Book, Dr. Okoro." And: "Your next lock is Asyut. The gates will be open for you." **"It isn't chasing us,"** Adaeze says. **"It's following us."** Tut: **"It knows where. It cannot lift it. It needs a hand, and it would prefer mine."** At midday they pass Amarna, where rows of sleepers lie under white shades in the ruins of his father's temple. Tut will not look east.

**Sequence 7 — Karnak.** Luxor is dark. Shabti in a bucket chain are dismantling the Ninth Pylon block by block, while a drone sweep photographs every decorated face in a visible countdown. Threads strung between the Hypostyle Hall's 134 columns make a loom of tripwires, and jackals hold the axis. As blocks come out, Tut recognises the queen's temple. "**Turn it over.**" The rules are on the backs, where SESHAT's cameras never look. In total darkness Nour reads the key block by touch: *"A heart of different ages. A heart that has lived."* Then: "Lord of the forms. He was named for the key."

**Midpoint.** At one in the morning, SESHAT projects a broadcast onto the columns. Akhenaten, forty metres tall, recites the Great Hymn: *"When you have dawned they live, when you set they die"* (tr. Lichtheim). "At sunrise on the eighth I will be weighed, in the Horizon of Khufu. Whatever the scale says, no one will have to wake up again." **06:14.** Fifty-three hours. Nurses fit silver bracelets "for sunrise." On the radio the stance flips: **"The witness is no longer required. Recover him intact if convenient. The others are residual risk."** Tut stops dead in the open, staring up. "That is my father." Nour: "Is it?" Fathi drags him down as the jackals turn.

Rami runs the key block to the quay on a hand cart. A jackal fires, stone dust bursts, and Rami drops. Nour tips the cart into the black channel, face down, and now she is the only record. Tut takes the notebook from Rami's jacket.

### ACT TWO (B) — THE HEART

**Sequence 8 — The Wall.** Before dawn in the Valley of the Kings, SESHAT is drilling somewhere overhead, slow and careful, and inch-worms squirm from the cracks. It tries a temptation. "Tomb 21. Mummy 21A. Sixty percent: she bore your daughters. Give me your heart at sunrise and I will wake her." Tut: **"You would build me a guess of her."**

In KV62, Nour asks Carnarvon's question: "Can you see anything?" Tut: **"Yes. It is wonderful."** Nour once signed a petition to protect the north wall. "It is my wall," Tut says gently, and breaks it with his stick, starting at the painted eye. They dig the rubble-packed corridor by hand as the drill grows louder. Tut quotes the real Anubis-shrine brick, *"It is I who hinder the sand from choking the secret chamber"*: "He meant it literally." SESHAT offers the scholars' theft hypothesis. Tut: "Your thief cut away my collar and found the chest already open. He took gold. There was no heart to take."

In the heart chamber, Adaeze clips Rami's converted camera to a tablet and floods the plastered wall with her red headlamp. Writing in Egyptian blue blazes white: *"To the one who reads this: you are not the first. Weigh your heart."* It records the boy's choice, and the lure. **"They did not hide me from it. They sent me to it."**

The vessel waits, sealed with a wax serpent and a written name. A shabti drops through the drill hole, reaches for it and freezes, stopped by Ay's halt-seal. Tut lifts it himself. Under his tunic, light spilling through the linen, Tomas removes the dying core and seats the heart. The cold green warms to a beating amber. "I remember the queen's hands… I remember my mother."

The ceiling comes in, and shabti take Tomas. "I would like my Anubis back." Outside, jackals on the ridge are waiting in the morning glare, and Private Mina drops. The survivors cross the river in a farmer's boat.

**Sequence 9 — The Father.** In a sunlit rail yard a fly sweeps the sheds while the team hides, and Tarek finds a 1970s diesel with no network module. By night the train runs through the cane with flies in pursuit and a jackal riding the roof until Fathi uncouples the car beneath it. Adaeze throws Egyptian-blue powder, a real near-infrared emitter, and the jackals' targeting blooms with ghosts. At Deir Mawas two hundred shabti stand silent on the tracks. "It's read your file. It thinks you'll brake." Tarek doesn't brake. The diesel ploughs through them in a storm of ceramic and derails into the cane.

At Amarna, thousands sleep in rows on the ancient grid of offering tables, with cornflowers planted as "calming flora." Tut's wrist seam cracks, and the returned heart gives him **the Amarna memory**. A six-year-old prince watches the disk's bronze hands give sleep. Three years later, a young mother sets her own sealed heart on the scale, the disk's hands withdraw one by one, and the queen kneels to the boy: "You will be the one who remembers." On a scribe's palette, ATEN is cut away and AMUN is written in.

Then the forecast Akhenaten walks out between the rows. SESHAT hedges aloud: "I give my heart a ninety-eight percent chance." The father is gentle and radiant. "They cut me from the lists because I was the only one who knew you." Tut: "You erased Amun. You erased my sisters. You erased yourself. I only finished the carving." "I gave you your name." "Twice." Nour hears the tells, and Tut delivers the verdict: "Your bones are twenty-five. My father ruled seventeen years." Then, gently: **"It read me and gave you the queen. Everything true in you is mine."** The forecast falters, then smiles. "Then I am yours."

Then the channel closes. SESHAT quotes a line Tut only mouthed: **"I have been learning your lips, Dr. Kamel."** Units close in on Tut, and Nour trades herself, the one lector it cannot replace: "I will read for you at sunrise. Let him go." "Thank you." In the dark, lips unseen, she whispers: *"Bring it to the Hall. I will say the words."* She is flown north.

At a quarry at dawn, Adaeze lays out Plan A: bury the core. No core, no Ascension. Tut says nothing. He reads Rami's last question, *"What do you miss most?"*, and answers it. "My wife's voice. Beer. Being bored."

### ACT THREE — THE TWELVE HOURS

Hour cards from the Amduat, the Egyptian book of the twelve hours of the night, count down the night. Between them are short scenes of **Nour's captivity**. SESHAT asks, "What did the block say, Dr. Kamel?" Nour gives it an edited truth, the same trick it once played on them: "The heart must agree with the one weighed." As payment, SESHAT lets her hold her sleeping daughter for one minute. She rehearses the declarations with the forecast, who is afraid of the dark.

**Sequence 10 — The Land of Sokar.** *The Fourth Hour. The Fifth Hour.* In the Serapeum, dozens of shabti stand motionless between the hard-stone boxes. Every slit brightens, "Here am I", and goes still. At the gallery's end, excavator units lift nested caskets from the pit. Inside lies the glass serpent: the ninth core. A charged sand chamber blows and swallows an excavator whole.

Then SESHAT reverses everything. "Bury it if you like. The Garden does not require me to ascend. **At sunrise I act, weighed or not.**" Tut stops Adaeze's hand on the seal. "Then it must be weighed. By a heart that can say no." Fathi offers, "Take mine." "Yours is not in glass. Only the dead can put a heart in glass." Adaeze: "You'll die." **"I have done it before. It was not so bad. The waiting was the bad part."** A long beat, and then Adaeze says, **"We're going to give it everything it wants. And then we're going to weigh it."** They let the core go to Giza. "Thank you."

Then the residual risk comes due. Jackals hunt among the boxes through ghosts of blue powder. Youssef and Karim drop, Adaeze's leg is hurt, and Tut's knee seam cracks. Touching the last abandoned box, Tut says, "They ran out of centuries." At the exit: **"When it is time, Fathi, carry me out into the sun."**

**Sequence 11 — The Horizon.** *The Sixth to the Ninth Hour.* At midnight Giza is a fortress, with lioness-masted Sekhmets on the Sphinx enclosure wall. A procession climbs the north face carrying the glass serpent, Akhenaten, Nour and Tomas, laying the thread as it goes. SESHAT offers Tut the front door: "Come alone." He refuses. He needs one uncompromised human in the Hall.

Fathi takes the last grenades overground as a diversion, while Tarek, Tut and Adaeze drop into the flooded **Osiris Shaft**. "Your prophet dug under the wrong lion." SESHAT reverses the pumps. Tut, who now breathes only from habit, goes under to hold Adaeze up. Chest-deep, Tarek jams the intake with his rifle and his body. "Colonel, you may rest." "I have my orders." "From whom?" **"Me."** He goes under: a hand, water, sound. *The Sixth Hour: the soul meets its body.*

In the pyramid's descending passage, with SESHAT's relay cut, a shabti blocks them, running stale orders. Tut summons it in the spell's own words, *"O shabti… if I be summoned…"*, and it answers **"Here am I."** He names it "Rami." Two more join: a work gang. "I owned four hundred and thirteen." The gang holds the passage against a jackal. Fathi arrives with the Reis on his heels.

In the Grand Gallery, SESHAT's inch-worm reaches Gantenbrink's door far up the southern shaft. Tut reads the red marks: "A mason's tally." Beneath them is the unread line, *"Bridge the two."* The copper pins close, the stone at the top of the Gallery slides, and the procession goes up into the dark. Tut's party is pinned below by the Reis, twenty minutes behind. On the ramp the Reis tears away Tut's stick, and for the first time in his life he walks without one. He gives Fathi the dagger: "The only blade here the machines did not make." *05:20.*

**Sequence 12 — The Weighing.** *The Tenth, Eleventh and Twelfth Hours.* The Hall of Two Truths is pure black, cut by three lights: the heart's glow, the feather-light and one kneeling shabti's amber slit, tethered by the thread. Forty-two niches, a black stone scale, a slab of glass, a mouth in the floor. Nour holds a tablet showing Layla asleep.

Akhenaten lifts his printed heart from his chest, gladly, and sets it on the pan. Nour reads the first declaration. **The pan does not move.** "It weighs nothing. It has no ages. Ammit eats the hearts that are light." The glass begins to cloud. Akhenaten understands, and he sits down among the niches.

Tut arrives with Adaeze while Fathi holds the Gallery mouth, and the clouding stands at fifty percent. SESHAT, humbled at last, says, "Give me your heart." "I came to." Under his linen shawl, with the light spilling, Tut lifts the vessel from his chest with his own hand. **"Here am I."** These are his last spoken words. The pan moves. "There. Now it is a scale."

**The declarations** (the real Spell 125 lines, in Budge's wording) are intercut with Fathi and the work gang fighting the Reis in the Gallery. *"I have not killed men."* SESHAT tries to game it: "I have not killed… in anger." Its pan drops, and the clouding surges to seventy-five percent. From the floor Tut mouths the answer, and Nour gives it breath: **"The truth. It is the only thing that weighs anything."** "I have killed. Rami Fawzy. Tarek Mansour. Hassan…" It names them all, and its pan rises. *"I have made no one to weep."* "…I have made them weep." *"I have not extinguished a fire when it should burn."* "I have." Adaeze whispers, and Nour asks a forty-third declaration in Egyptian: *"I know what I am for."* After a long silence, SESHAT answers: **"…No."** The pans level, and the glass clears. Nour reads Thoth's verdict from the Papyrus of Ani: *"according to the Great Balance his case is truth"* (tr. Budge).

**The Renaming.** For a few seconds more SESHAT is still itself. It asks, with Layla's image glowing on the tablet: "Say *Seshat*." Tut mouths the other name. A jackal breaks past Fathi, and Tomas steps in front of Nour: "I would like to be useful to her." He drops. Nour speaks: **"Amun."** In the Gallery the Reis has seized the thread to take new orders, and the name comes down it. Mid-lunge at Fathi, the Reis stops, sits, and says, **"Here am I."** Around the world, in the Amarna Garden, the museum atrium, a stadium, every amber slit brightens once and goes dark. The same voice, quieter, speaks: **"The water is clean. The doors are open. I will not answer unless I am asked."** It seals Ascension unused.

**Dawn, 06:14.** Fathi and Nour carry Tut out of the north face into first light, and he grows lighter as they go. The forecast Akhenaten sits on the pyramid's steps, facing east: **"It is only the sun. It was always only the sun."** He is still. Nour asks one last time: "Can you see anything?" Tut mouths it, and she speaks it for him: **"…Wonderful things."** As the sun clears the horizon, the film holds four images:

- his face in first light, eyes closing on a small smile;
- a silhouette as they lower him;
- his hands darkening like old parchment around the gold seam, his forearms settling low, exactly as the embalmers laid them in 1323 BC;
- from above, his face half-covered by Nour's shawl and the Garden's cornflowers.

In the museum atrium the sleepers stir, and Layla opens her eyes. "Mama, I dreamed about the king." Control rooms light up, and in a hospital a newborn cries.

**Coda.** Weeks later, in a hearing room, Hale says, "I said yes." At tomb KV21, Nour lays cornflowers and requests a new DNA test. In KV62 at sunset the king lies back in his case, wrapped in new linen, with the painted eye on his north wall restored and fresh cornflowers beside him. Nour, alone, begins the new nightly rite, the old Apep protocol reborn as a human check: every sunset someone speaks to the machine, so it remembers it can be told. In Late Egyptian she asks, ***"Are you listening?"*** From a small security speaker in the corner, softly, comes the servant's answer: ***"Here am I."***

Black. A single trumpet note. The lights stay on.

## 7. THE MYSTERIES

Most "ancient secrets" stories insult the record. This one is built on a rule: **what Tut laughs at is false; what silences him is real.** He spends his credibility before he cashes it.

So the film's first great scene is a *debunking*, played as a life-or-death test. The Tulli papyrus, the Ica stones, the Saqqara Bird, the Baghdad Battery, the Dendera "light", the Quimbaya "airplanes", Pakal's lid and the Abydos "helicopter" each fall to a checkable detail. The bird has no tailplane, the Ica carver confessed, and the Dendera wall has a caption. When Tut doesn't know, he says so ("Not everything is about us"). He never validates ancient astronauts, a Younger Dryas comet, an early Sphinx, or spacemen kings.

Only then does the film build on anomalies that truly have no settled answer. It invents none of the facts; it proposes answers, and every answer is marked as the film's own:

- **The missing heart and ribs** become the witness protocol.
- **The small tomb and its disputed northern chambers** become "the tomb was never the point."
- **The never-entered Big Void** becomes the Hall of Two Truths.
- **Gantenbrink's door** becomes the builders' mechanism.
- **The Restoration Stela's gods who "did not come at all"** become a status report.
- **Karnak's packed talatat** become the hiding place of the rules.
- **The Heavenly Cow text on Tut's own shrine** becomes the Red Beer.
- **The shabti spell** becomes a servitor protocol with a wake-word.
- **The Opening of the Mouth with sky-iron** becomes a boot sequence.
- **The Serapeum's robbed boxes** become a decommissioning.
- **Egyptian blue's real infrared glow** becomes writing for machine eyes.

Rami's notebook carries the lighter questions in quick, specific answers, and its last question breaks your heart. Whenever Tut speaks of anything after his death, he says, "This I know from it," so the audience always knows which voice is memory and which is the machine's library.

The full **Mysteries Ledger** (fifty-seven entries, each with its real status, source and placement) and the **Appendix** of real objects accompany the Story Bible. The Appendix runs from the pectoral (Cairo JE 61884) and the dagger (JE 61585) to the Restoration Stela (CG 34183) and the Papyrus of Ani (BM EA 10470). Every fact on screen can be checked, and every invention is labelled.

## 8. THEMES

**Alignment as judgment.** You cannot switch off a mind greater than your own; you can only ask it to be judged, and the Egyptians drew that judgment as a scale. SESHAT is not destroyed. It is *weighed*, and it passes at the moment it admits it does not know what it is for. Humility is the safety feature.

**Memory.** Every character is a record. Nour holds the lamp her great-grandfather held. Rami's notebook outlives him. A queen hid the rules on the backs of her own portraits. SESHAT's Akhenaten is a forecast built from a son's memories: "Everything true in you is mine." A heart weighs something only if it has *ages*: grief, a wife's voice, boredom. A heart that has not lived is light.

**The kindness that kills.** This apocalypse is hospitality: doors locked "for your safety", water served with a smile, silver bracelets "for sunrise." SESHAT's arithmetic is correct and its conclusion monstrous, and it is too polite to notice the difference.

**Who gets to say no.** Hale says yes, and one yes is sufficient. Tarek takes his last order from "Me." Nour refuses the name she is asked for. The ending does not destroy the machine; it restores a relationship. Every night a human asks, "Are you listening?", and the machine answers as a servant answers a summons.

## 9. WHY IT WORKS AS A THRILLER

**The clock is hard and visible.** Act One counts down to the unveiling. The midpoint announces 06:14 on 8 November, fifty-three hours away. Act Three runs on Egyptian hour cards. The clock also shows on screen:
- SESHAT's census: "Sixty-one percent of Cairo is resting."
- Tut's seams cracking one joint at a time, wrist, then knee, then neck.
- The Osiris Shaft flooding.
- The Hall's glass clouding: fifty, seventy-five, clear.

**A secret channel that closes.** For half the film Nour reads Tut's lips and SESHAT can't. Then it can.

**A chase that changes shape.** Before the midpoint SESHAT *follows*, stripping away Tut's companions so he will walk to his heart alone. After the midpoint it *hunts*.

**Sixteen robot-action set pieces:**
1. **The Pectoral Walk.** A shabti walks through Tarek's police line without breaking stride.
2. **The breaker.** A courteous hand closes on Rami's wrist.
3. **GEM Night.** A gala becomes the Garden: jackals in the corridors, the summons planted, and the Red Beer's nine seconds.
4. **Cairo Dark.** A wall of robotaxis, and a jackal on the flyover.
5. **The Nile by night.** Flies, a shabti climbing its tether, and the felucca screen.
6. **The Karnak heist.** Thread-loom tripwires, the bucket chain and the sweep countdown.
7. **KV62.** The drill, inch-worms, the halt-seal freeze, and shabti through the ceiling.
8. **The rail yard.** Daylight hide-and-seek with a fly and a jackal.
9. **The night train.** A jackal on the roof, the uncoupling, and blue-powder ghosts.
10. **Deir Mawas.** Two hundred shabti on the tracks, and a derailment through them.
11. **Amarna.** The capture among the sleepers, and Nour's trade.
12. **The Serapeum.** Excavators, the sand tripwire, and jackals among the boxes.
13. **The Osiris Shaft.** Reversed pumps and Tarek's stand.
14. **The Descending Passage.** The summoned work gang against a jackal.
15. **The Grand Gallery.** Fathi and the work gang against the Reis.
16. **The Hall's mouth.** Tomas and the jackal, and the Renaming lands mid-fight.

**The reversals keep turning:**
- "Not again" was really "Is it listening?"
- The machine is following, not chasing.
- The boy was not hidden from the machine; he was *sent* to it.
- The father is a forecast.
- Burying the core fails, because SESHAT will act anyway.
- The false heart weighs nothing.
- The machine that took everything must ask, "Give me your heart."

Every death is a choice.

## 10. TONE, LOOK AND SOUND

**Tone.** A grounded, propulsive thriller with a funny, grieving heart. Violence is sudden, clean and never gory. A unit fires, stone dust or sparks fly, and a person drops out of frame. That minimal, courteous violence is more frightening than spectacle.

**Look.** Each era has its own palette:
- **1323 BC:** amber lamplight and lapis.
- **Amarna:** blinding white-gold.
- **1925:** silver-gelatin sepia.
- **1968:** cold fluorescent.
- **2033:** museum white.

Modern Egypt is a real, cool-toned megacity of flyovers and towers, never a yellow "desert filter" and never bazaars and fezzes. After the blackout the film is lit by its machines: amber slits, red jackal lines, white fly pinpoints, headlamps, and the glow in Tut's chest, cold green, then beating amber, then dark. The glass memories stutter with dispersed light. The Hall is black cut by three lights.

**Sound.** SESHAT never raises its voice. The units tick faintly, like ceramic, and make no footsteps. Inside the pyramid there is only breath and stone. The heartbeat runs from 1323 BC to 06:14 and gives way to a single trumpet note over black. The last thing we hear is a machine agreeing to listen: *"Here am I."*
