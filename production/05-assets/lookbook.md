# VISUAL LOOKBOOK — LOCKED
## We're From The Future · S01E01 "The Package"

**2.00:1 · 24 fps · spherical · naturalistic**

---

## 1. THE ONE RULE

> **The house is photographed like a marriage. The leaf is photographed like a guest.**

Everything below is an elaboration of that sentence. If a shot decision doesn't
serve it, it's wrong.

---

## 2. THE THREE LIGHT STATES

The episode has exactly three lighting worlds and moves through them in order.
There is no fourth. A shot that mixes them without story reason is a continuity
failure, not a style choice.

### STATE A — HOUSEHOLD (teaser → end of Act One)
Warm tungsten practicals. Two table lamps, one overhead in the kitchen, one hall
fixture. Everything is motivated by a bulb you can see or point at.

- Key: 3200K, soft, low and side-on. Faces modelled from one side.
- Fill: bounce off the walls — the room does the work.
- Contrast: gentle. Shadows are warm, never black.
- Feeling: this is a place where people have eaten a thousand dinners.

### STATE B — DIVIDED (Act Two → Act Three)
The pane becomes a light source. From the moment it wakes in the living room,
every shot in that room has two colour temperatures in it.

- Warm practical on one side of the face, cold pane on the other.
- **The two colours meet ON THE SKIN.** Not on the wall behind. The character
  is the boundary.
- The colder the shot gets, the further the actors are from each other.
- Feeling: something has been introduced into the ecosystem.

### STATE C — DISPLACED (Act Four blackout → end)
The practicals are gone. The pane is the only light in the house.

- Single source, low, front-and-below. The angle of a screen, not of a fire.
- 6000K, flat, with almost no falloff — it does not behave like a lamp.
- **Faces must stay legible.** Depth of black is a grade decision; losing an eye
  is not. If a performance is unreadable, the shot is unusable, full stop.
- Feeling: they are being lit by something that is looking at them.

**The transition points are dramatic, not technical:**
State A → B at the moment the leaf first wakes in the living room (11:00-ish).
State B → C at the blackout in Act Four.

## 3. LENS LANGUAGE

| Range | Use | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 24–28mm | The two exteriors. The final shot. | Isolation. The house is small and the street doesn't care. |
| 35mm | Wide two-shots inside the house | Shows the room *and* the couple. The relationship lens. |
| 40–50mm | Medium two-shots and singles | The episode's home. Neutral, observational, no comment. |
| 65–75mm | Close-ups | Close enough to read a lie, far enough not to invade. |
| 85–100mm | Inserts, and the street POVs | Compresses. Flattens. Makes the ordinary look examined. |
| 135mm | The opening shot only | Establishes the show's grammar: we are watching from a distance, and we are patient. |

**Stop:** T2.0–T2.8 for interiors. Shallow but not fashionable — one face sharp,
the other soft, and the choice of which is a story decision every time.

**Never:** zooms, snap-focus, whip pans, dutch angles, lens flares, drone moves.

## 4. CAMERA MOVEMENT — the escalation ladder

The camera's behaviour is a graph of Miles and Lauren's stability. It has four
rungs and it climbs them once.

1. **Locked off** (teaser). No movement at all. The camera is comfortable.
2. **Very slow push** (Act One onward). 15–25mm of travel over 8 seconds. The
   audience should feel it and not be able to point at it. Reserved for the
   moment a character understands something.
3. **Barely-perceptible handheld** (Acts Two–Three). Operator breathing, no more.
   Used *only* on the person losing control of the conversation.
4. **Restrained handheld** (three shots total: the Act Three shout, Miles
   crossing to the pane, the Act Four camera roll). Never a fourth.

**The rule:** the camera never moves faster than the characters do. There is no
shot in this episode where the camera is more agitated than the person in it.

## 5. FRAMING THE MARRIAGE

The single most disciplined thing in the episode.

| Segment | Framing |
|---|---|
| Teaser | Shared frames. They are in the same shot more often than not. |
| Act One | Still shared, but the box is between them in the frame. |
| Act Two | Singles begin. The doorway separates them. |
| Act Three | **Hard separation.** From the fight onward they are not in the same frame. |
| Act Four | Separate rooms, separate frames, cut together. |
| Act Five | Separate — until the last shot. |
| **Final shot** | **Shared frame. Shoulder to shoulder.** |

**The discipline is not "no two-shots" — Act Four requires them working the
problem together, and separating them there would be a lie about the marriage.
The discipline is PHYSICAL CONTACT.**

Miles and Lauren touch exactly **three times** in thirty-six minutes:

| # | Runtime | Shot | The touch |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | 02:10 | SH0032 | He hides his face in her shoulder rather than be photographed |
| 2 | 22:15 | SH0211 | His hand on her arm in the near-repair — **interrupted by the leaf** |
| 3 | **34:57** | **SH0313** | **Shoulder against shoulder in the final wide** |

The first is a refusal wearing affection. The second is aborted. The third is the
last shot of the episode. **It cost them the entire hour to stand next to each
other**, and the audience feels that without being told, because the body memory
of two people not touching for twelve minutes is real.

`tools/build.py` asserts there are exactly three contact shots and fails the
build otherwise, so a fourth touch cannot drift into the cut.

**Through the fight itself (19:28 → 21:58) they do share frames — but always on
the widest lens in the room, at opposite ends of it.** Sharing a frame while
being as far apart as the room allows is a more painful image than not sharing
one at all.

**Sub-rule — Miles's blocking:** in nine separate scenes Miles physically places
himself between Lauren and a door, window, or the leaf. Never remarked on, never
covered in a way that flatters it. On rewatch it is the most loving thing in the
episode and the most damaging.

## 6. HOW TO SHOOT THE LEAF

The hardest thing in the show. Get this wrong and the episode is a gadget video.

**Photograph it as an object, not a screen.**
- It gets the coverage a person gets: singles, inserts, an eyeline.
- It is almost always shot **flat-on or from slightly above**. Never a low
  hero angle. It is not majestic; it is *present*.
- Its edge is its most important feature. Rake light along it to prove there is
  no bezel, no port, no seam.

**Four physical rules, visible on camera:**
1. Reflections lag by half a second. Establish it once, in the kitchen, clean and
   unambiguous, and never explain it again.
2. Temperature is inverted. Shot through behaviour — hands, breath, a cold brass
   drawer pull — never through an effect.
3. Its mass moves. Tilt it, and the weight follows a beat late.
4. It does not respond to Miles.

**Forbidden, absolutely:** glow bloom, particles, holograms, scan lines, boot
sequences, animated UI, logos, progress bars, any interface element that a
designer would enjoy. **The leaf has no user interface. It has a face, four
words, a number, and a photograph. That is its entire vocabulary.**

**The reflection lag is the show's signature image.** It costs nothing and it
tells an attentive viewer the truth twenty minutes before anyone says it: this is
not a screen. It is a window, and there is a room on the other side.

## 7. COLOUR

| | |
|---|---|
| Warm side | Tungsten amber, 3200K, gently desaturated. Skin sits slightly warm. |
| Cold side | 6000–6500K, **very low saturation**. Almost neutral. Not blue — *drained*. |
| Blacks | Lifted a touch, with a faint warm toe. Never crushed. |
| Saturation | Overall low. Reds are the only colour permitted to be vivid, and they appear only in the teaser. |

**The colour arc:** the episode drains. The teaser is the most saturated it ever
gets; the last shot is nearly monochrome. Do this gradually, across acts, so
nobody can point at the moment it happened.

**Forbidden:** teal-and-orange, day-for-night blue wash, coloured practicals,
any hue that isn't in the room.

---

## 8. TEXTURE

- **Grain:** fine, consistent, present in every frame including the darkest.
  Grain is the show's promise that this was photographed.
- **Sharpness:** no digital sharpening anywhere. Faces slightly soft is correct;
  faces crisp is wrong.
- **Halation:** a very small amount on the pane's white type only. Nowhere else.
- **Skin:** pores, flyaway hair, the flush across Miles's cheeks, the loose
  strands at Lauren's temples. If skin looks like a render, the shot is rejected.

---

## 9. NEGATIVE SPACE AND COMPOSITION

- **Headroom is generous in the teaser and tightens across the episode.** By Act
  Five the frames are close and the ceilings are low.
- Doorways, hallways, and the frame-within-a-frame of the front door's frosted
  panel are the episode's recurring compositional motif. **Someone is almost
  always framed by an opening.**
- The front door is visible, deep in frame, in as many living-room wides as
  possible from Act Three onward. Nobody ever mentions it. By Act Five the
  audience is watching it without being told to.
- Empty space on the side of frame a character is looking toward = safety.
  Empty space *behind* them = the opposite. Flip this at the blackout.

## 10. DELIVERY FORMATS

| Format | Use | Rule |
|---|---|---|
| **2.00:1** | Master | Composed for. The only true frame. |
| 16:9 | Broadcast/social | Protect: extract centre. Never reframe a two-shot into a single. |
| 9:16 | Vertical promo | **Only shots already composed centrally.** The final wide and every two-shot are forbidden in vertical — they cannot survive it and shouldn't be forced to. |

Safe-action and safe-title guides are cut for all three at the previs stage, and
the shot list flags every frame that has no valid vertical extraction.
