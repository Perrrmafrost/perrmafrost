# PHASE 2 — STORY DEVELOPMENT
## Three Episode-1 structures, compared, one chosen and improved

---

# OPTION A — "THE LONG EVENING"
### Strict real-time. One location. No flashback.

The entire episode occurs across ~90 continuous minutes of story time in the
house, compressed to 32. No cutaways except the post-credit tag. The package
arrives at the end of the teaser and never leaves the frame for long.

**Beat spine**
1. Teaser — domestic evening. Doorbell.
2. Box on the table. Neither will open it. They open it.
3. The leaf. The impossible details accumulate. The eye.
4. Miles tries to make it a malfunction. Lauren refuses. It speaks.
5. The fight. The confession. The countdown.
6. Phenomena. The clue. The phrase from childhood.
7. Lights. Lock. Knock.

**Strengths**
- Maximum intimacy and dread. The house becomes a pressure vessel.
- Cheapest possible production: one location, two actors, one prop.
- The real-time countdown is structurally native.

**Weaknesses**
- Risk of staginess by Act 4. Two people and a table for 32 minutes needs
  extraordinary blocking discipline.
- No visual relief. The eye tires.
- Withholding *all* backstory imagery can read as evasion rather than mystery.

# OPTION B — "TWO TIMES"
### Intercut present with the night of the crossing.

Alternating structure. Present-day evening intercut throughout with a
non-linear, fragmentary account of the night Miles and Lauren arrived — rain,
a motel, a third person, a decision. The flashbacks resolve just enough by Act 5
to reframe the knock.

**Beat spine**
1. Teaser — domestic. Doorbell. / FLASH: headlights on wet asphalt.
2. The box. / FLASH: a room with three coats on the bed.
3. The eye. / FLASH: Miles alone, writing something on paper tape.
4. The fight. / FLASH: someone's hand letting go of Lauren's.
5. Countdown. / FLASH: the motel sign. Miles looking back.
6. The clue is the motel. Lauren has never seen it.
7. Knock. "It's me." / FLASH: the third coat.

**Strengths**
- Visual variety. Solves Option A's fatigue problem entirely.
- Answers "who is Miles writing to" with an *image* rather than a line.
- Strongest possible Episode 2 launch — we've already seen the motel.

**Weaknesses**
- **It answers too much.** By showing the crossing, the pilot spends its
  deepest mystery in its first hour. Season 1 has nowhere to go.
- Halves the domestic runtime, which is where the marriage lives. The couple
  becomes less real, and the couple *is* the show.
- Doubles the production package: second location, period/weather work, a third
  character who must be cast and hidden.
- Undercuts the in-world withholding conceit: if the camera can go back, the
  characters' refusal to speak about it becomes decorative.

# OPTION C — "THE REPLY"
### Real-time house, plus a controlled breach: what the leaf shows.

Option A's discipline, with one deliberate exception. The camera never leaves
the house — but **the leaf does.** Twice, for a total of under twenty seconds,
we see through it: footage of Miles and Lauren somewhere impossible, and later a
still image of a place. The leaf is the episode's only window, and it is
unreliable.

Crucially, Option C adds the structural engine Options A and B lack:
**the package is a reply, not a delivery.**

**Beat spine**
1. **Teaser (3:30)** — Domestic evening. Locks checked. Hooks empty. Smoke
   detector chirps. "We're not from around here." Doorbell. Neither ordered
   anything.
2. **Act One (5:30)** — The box is too clean. The label has a crossed seven in
   Lauren's abandoned handwriting. They open it. The leaf: no power, no ports,
   inverted temperature, a reflection half a second late. It wakes for less than
   a second. An eye. It dies. Miles's face gives him away.
3. **Act Two (6:30)** — Miles argues malfunction, disposal. Lauren refuses. The
   argument exposes an old promise — fragments of the Terms surface for the first
   time. The leaf wakes on its own. A woman with Lauren's face and voice,
   perfectly still, addresses Lauren — and watches Miles. She knows things. She
   says their staying has consequences. She cuts out mid-sentence into three
   seconds of footage: the two of them, older, wrong, elsewhere. Lauren
   recognizes something. Miles says he doesn't. Hard cut to silence.
4. **Act Three (6:30)** — Containment fails, quietly. Miles admits he knows what
   the leaf is. Lauren gets the smaller truth and can feel the shape of the
   larger one. **The turn:** Lauren reads the label date and understands it is
   the date on the calendar — the date Miles sends. *"You didn't get a package.
   You got an answer."* The fight. The near-repair. The leaf interrupts with
   four words in the exact cadence of the Terms. The countdown starts.
5. **Act Four (6:30)** — Small impossibilities: a timestamp that hasn't happened,
   a photograph nobody took, a clock that steps back four seconds, the smoke
   detector's chirp answered from the wrong room. They work the problem like
   professionals. The leaf gives them a place. Then it says a sentence from
   Lauren's childhood that she has never spoken aloud here, and calls her by a
   name we do not hear. Lights out. The lock turns itself.
6. **Act Five (5:00)** — They prepare, competently, to leave. Miles gives up the
   last partial truth: nothing crosses over whole. Lauren asks whether the
   message came from her. He can't answer. The leaf's final line. Three knocks,
   then two. **"It's me."** Miles's face. Black.
7. **Tag (0:35)** — A pale room. A file. LOST → LOCATED. The second file already
   reads LOCATED, dated years ago.

**Strengths**
- Keeps Option A's intimacy and its production economy (one location, two
  performers, one hero prop, plus two short breach sequences).
- Solves Option A's visual fatigue with two precisely-budgeted breaches instead
  of a whole second timeline.
- The **"it's a reply"** turn gives Act Three a genuine reversal, which A lacks
  and B buries. It converts the pilot from *"something found us"* into
  *"you called them,"* which is a far better engine because it is a marriage
  problem wearing a science-fiction coat.
- Preserves every load-bearing mystery. Nothing is spent.
- The countdown makes Acts 4–5 propulsive without a chase.
- "It's me" pays off the reply structure in three syllables.

**Weaknesses (and mitigations)**
- The two breaches must be *flawless* or they become the AI-slop moment the
  brief forbids. → Mitigation: hold both under 4 seconds, degrade them
  deliberately, never show a face clearly, and cut on movement.
- Real-time countdown constrains editorial. → Mitigation: lock the countdown to
  the EDL as generated data, not hand-drawn art. (Implemented in `tools/`.)

# DECISION

**OPTION C — "THE REPLY" is selected.**

Rationale in one line: *A is the best-behaved, B is the most spectacular, C is
the only one where the science fiction and the marriage are the same problem.*

## Improvements applied to Option C before scripting

1. **The crossed seven.** Originally the label simply bore Lauren's handwriting.
   Too blunt — handwriting recognition is a thriller cliché. Replaced with a
   habit she *abandoned:* she used to cross her sevens and stopped, deliberately,
   because nobody here does. The label's seven is crossed. It is not evidence
   that she wrote it. It is evidence that somebody knew her *before she started
   hiding.* Planted in the teaser as a gesture she aborts mid-stroke.

2. **"DO NOT GO BACK" is not the horror — its grammar is.** Original beat had
   Miles react to the content. Weak; the content is obvious. Now he reacts to the
   *phrasing*, because it is Clause Three verbatim. The message came from
   somewhere that has the Terms. Lauren watches him realize it and does not
   understand yet.

3. **The countdown is bound to the episode clock.** It was decoration. Now it
   runs in real time and reaches zero at the knock — so the pilot's open question
   gets an answer in the last thirty seconds, and the answer is a person.

4. **The double's eyeline.** Added: she addresses Lauren and tracks Miles.
   Costs nothing, changes everything on second viewing.

5. **The final line reversed.** The double was written as a warning ("you were
   supposed to disappear"). Now she is the version who *obeyed* — who left when
   she was told — and she is warning them not to. She is not a threat. She is
   evidence of what compliance costs. This makes the scariest character in the
   pilot also the saddest, which is the tone the series wants.

6. **"It's me" replaces "open the door."** Threat is generic. Intimacy is not.

7. **The tag's second file.** Originally one status change. Now two files: Miles
   flips LOST → LOCATED, and Lauren's already says LOCATED with an old date.
   One image, and the audience re-reads the entire episode.
